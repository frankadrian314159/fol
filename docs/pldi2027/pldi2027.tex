\begin{abstract}
Persistent-first languages pay a large allocation tax on accumulation loops. Languages like Koka eliminate it with ownership types (Perceus/FBIP); Clojure asks the programmer to opt in manually with transients. We show the optimization can be made \emph{automatic and sound} in a latently-typed language with open-world CLOS dispatch and live redefinition—with no type system—via (1) a tail-position-sensitive \emph{usage classification} that establishes uniqueness-at-death of loop and fold accumulators, (2) a rewriter aligned with the classifier \emph{by construction}, targeting edit-tagged in-place transients, and (3) \emph{world-guarded regions} that keep the optimization sound when definitions change at runtime. The result: 3.5–4.5× wall-time and 8.7–11× allocation improvements on accumulation-bound loops—closing 74–80\% of the gap to handwritten mutable Common Lisp—1.7–3.5× on whole programs such as word-frequency counting, byte-identical outputs on a nontrivial event simulator, zero measured cost when disabled or inapplicable, and an honest cost model of when transient conversion pays.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Languages built on persistent data structures offer powerful guarantees of immutability and thread safety, but they often pay a significant performance penalty known as the "persistence tax." Every update creates a new version of the data structure, allocating new path nodes from the root. In tight accumulation loops, this leads to excessive allocation and garbage collection pressure.
 
In the language FOL (a Lisp dialect with persistent data structures), the cost is stark. Compared to handwritten, mutable Common Lisp, simple accumulation loops run up to 114× slower (55× more memory). The cost is especially severe for loops that thread an accumulator through user-defined helper functions; our Derived Value Invalidation (DVI) benchmark, which follows this pattern, is 20× slower in time but suffers \textbf{900×} higher memory use due to O(N²) GC pressure. This paper presents a sound, automatic optimization that eliminates the allocation tax for a large class of \emph{intraprocedural} accumulation loops. We use the interprocedural DVI case not as a problem this paper solves, but as a clear boundary condition that delineates the scope of our technique and motivates the future work required to cross it.

The canonical pattern suffering from this tax is the accumulation loop, common in both \texttt{loop/recur} forms and \texttt{reduce} folds:

\begin{lstlisting}
;; Loop-based accumulation
(loop [acc {}]
  ...
  (recur (assoc acc k v)))

;; Fold-based accumulation
(reduce (fn [acc x] (conj acc ...))
        [] coll)
\end{lstlisting}

In each iteration, the call to \texttt{assoc} or \texttt{conj} allocates a new persistent map or vector, immediately discarding the previous version. The key insight is that if the old \texttt{acc} is used for nothing other than producing the new \texttt{acc} (i.e., it is unique at its point of death), the update can be performed in-place.

Existing solutions are not directly transferable to a language like FOL. Perceus/FBIP \cite{reinking2021perceus} and its relatives \cite{lorenzen2023functional} rely on ownership and uniqueness types, which are absent in our dynamically-typed language. Clojure \cite{hickey2009clojure} provides a manual, unsafe escape hatch: the programmer can explicitly opt into \texttt{transient} data structures and \texttt{assoc!}-style in-place updates, but misuse can lead to corrupted data and spooky action at a distance. Furthermore, FOL supports live redefinition of functions, meaning the semantic assumptions baked into an optimized loop might be invalidated \emph{while that code is running}.

This paper presents a fully automatic and sound system for converting persistent accumulation loops to use in-place transients. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Uniqueness-at-death by usage classification.} A flow- and tail-position-sensitive classification of accumulator uses (\texttt{:recur-update}, \texttt{:exit-bare}, etc.) that establishes when a persistent accumulator may be converted to an in-place transient—without ownership or linear types. Includes chain recognition through \texttt{->} threads, if-branches, and \emph{reduce init-threading} (a fold that linearly threads the accumulator is itself a chain link).
    \item \textbf{Alignment-by-construction between analysis and transformation.} The classifier refuses to qualify anything the rewriter cannot reach (\texttt{:recur\_in\_complex\_context}) or that would change representation mid-loop (\texttt{:recur\_reset}); per-representation \emph{op gates} ensure converted code can never hit an unsupported operation at runtime.
    \item \textbf{Name-resolution-faithful, two-tier summaries.} The Tier-1 standard-library summary table matches operators exactly the way the compiler itself resolves them (by name, with per-compilation-unit exclusions)—the analysis is no less sound than the language's own resolution model. Tier-2 extends coverage to user functions by \emph{inferring} summaries over the same effect lattice with a bounded fixed-point that handles self- and mutual recursion; inferred summaries are invalidated on redefinition (§\ref{sec:tier2}).
    \item \textbf{Soundness under live redefinition without world-age infrastructure.} Converted regions compile to guarded dual paths; validity cells register at load time indexed by assumed names; definitions notify at execution time and invalidate exactly their dependents. Guards measure as free.
    \item \textbf{An honest empirical cost model.} We quantify when transient conversion is profitable. Wrapper transients (O(n) boundaries) win only at large ops-per-boundary; our edit-tagged transients (O(1)/O(32) boundaries) extend the win to small-N accumulations and read-heavy folds. We validate this with benchmarks, including one (DVI) whose accumulation flows through a user-defined helper and therefore sits beyond the conversion client's reach---delineating exactly where the alignment discipline draws the line (§8)---and an end-to-end system whose hot loop is bounded by factors this technique does not address.
\end{enumerate}

Our evaluation shows 3.5--4.5× wall-time and 8.7--11× allocation improvements on accumulation-bound microbenchmarks, closing 74--80\% of the time gap to handwritten mutable Common Lisp; 1.7--3.5× on whole programs (quicksort, word-frequency counting), with the inconvertible style of the same quicksort correctly refused; byte-identical results on a complex event simulator; and negligible overhead when the optimization is disabled or inapplicable (§\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{Background and a Motivating Walk-through}

\textbf{FOL in one column.} FOL is a Lisp-1 dialect transpiled to Common Lisp. Its core data structures are persistent. Vectors are 32-way tries with a mutable tail for efficient appends, similar to Clojure's. Dictionaries are Hash Array Mapped Tries (HAMTs) \cite{bagwell2001ideal}. \texttt{(loop [bindings] ... (recur new-vals))} provides tail-call-optimized looping. The \texttt{->} macro threads an expression as the first argument to a series of forms. \texttt{reduce} applies a function to an accumulator and successive items from a collection. The compiler transpiles forms one by one. Calls to standard library functions are resolved by name at emit time against a known list, a fact that is load-bearing for our analysis (§3.3).

\textbf{Worked Example.} Consider \texttt{lh-pop-batch}, a function from the LSim event simulator. It builds up a new priority queue \texttt{acc} by repeatedly \texttt{conj}'ing items, then returns it inside a tuple literal \texttt{[acc q]}.

\emph{Original Code:}
\begin{lstlisting}
(defn lh-pop-batch [q n]
  (loop [acc [] i 0]
    (if (< i n)
      (let [[v new-q] (pop q)]
        (recur (conj acc v) (inc i)))
      [acc q]))) ; Exit with acc in a literal
\end{lstlisting}
Each call to \texttt{conj} creates a new persistent vector, copying the path from the root. Our analysis identifies \texttt{acc} as a candidate. It verifies that \texttt{acc} is freshly allocated (\texttt{[]}), never escapes the loop, and its only uses are being updated in the \texttt{recur} call (a sanctioned update) and being returned in a literal at the exit (a sanctioned exit).

\emph{Converted Code (Simplified):}
\begin{lstlisting}
(defn lh-pop-batch [q n]
  (if (is-valid? *lh-pop-batch-region-1*)
    ;; Fast path
    (loop [acc-t (transient []) i 0]
      (if (< i n)
        (let [[v new-q] (pop q)]
          (recur (conj! acc-t v) (inc i)))
        [(persistent! acc-t) q]))
    ;; Slow/fallback path
    (loop [acc [] i 0]
      ... ; Original code
      )))
\end{lstlisting}
The compiler emits a dual-path version. The fast path initializes a \texttt{transient} vector. The update uses the mutating \texttt{conj!} operation. At the exit, \texttt{persistent!} converts the transient back to a persistent vector in O(1) time before embedding it in the result. A guard, \texttt{is-valid?}, checked once on entry, ensures this transformation is safe. If a function like \texttt{conj} were redefined, the guard would fail, and execution would transparently use the original, safe code path.

\textbf{Clojure Transient Recap.} Clojure's transients provide a mutable view over a persistent collection. Updates are applied in-place to the transient version. Reads are problematic: a read from a transient may or may not see an in-place update, depending on the representation. "Wrapper" transients forbid reads entirely. "Edit-tagged" transients, which we use, support reads by navigating a mix of original and copied-on-write nodes, making them suitable for a wider range of algorithms, like read-heavy folds.

\section{The Analysis}

Our goal is to prove \emph{uniqueness-at-death}: the accumulator value at each iteration is used for no other purpose than to produce the value for the next iteration, and the final value does not share its identity with any other live object. This is a stronger property than standard escape analysis, which only determines if a value \emph{may} escape a region.

\subsection{Two Properties: Escape vs. Uniqueness-at-Death}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Escape (May-analysis):} An object \emph{escapes} if its lifetime may exceed its static scope. Standard escape analysis tracks this to enable stack allocation.
    \item \textbf{Uniqueness-at-Death (Must-analysis):} An object is \emph{unique at death} if, at the point of its last use, no other aliases to it exist. This requires:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Freshness:} The initial value is a fresh allocation not aliased elsewhere.
        \item \textbf{No-aliasing:} Inside the loop, no new aliases are created that outlive the current iteration (e.g., by storing the accumulator in a global or captured variable).
        \item \textbf{Last-use:} The \texttt{recur} or \texttt{reduce} update is the final use of the value in the current iteration.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

If these hold, the object's identity is not observable, and in-place mutation is safe.

\subsection{Tier-1 Summaries: The Effect Lattice}

To analyze the behavior of functions called within the loop, we use a summary-based analysis. Tier-1 summaries cover the standard library. Each summary classifies a function's effect on its arguments, particularly the accumulator. We define an effect lattice:

\texttt{:none} $<$ \texttt{:invoked} $<$ \texttt{:shared-with-result} $<$ \texttt{:retained}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{:none}: The argument is not used.
    \item \texttt{:invoked}: The argument is called as a function but not retained.
    \item \texttt{:shared-with-result}: The argument may be part of the function's return value (e.g., \texttt{(identity acc)}).
    \item \texttt{:retained}: The argument escapes to a persistent location (e.g., \texttt{(add-watch! acc ...)}). This is a disqualifier.
\end{itemize}

We hand-verified ~75 summaries for core functions. A key insight is the \textbf{root-level convention}: persistent operations like \texttt{assoc} and \texttt{conj} are summarized as returning a fresh root that may reference its (unchanged) children. Because our transient protocol is copy-on-write and never mutates elements, this summary is sound for all clients, including transient ones.

\subsection{Name-Resolution Faithfulness}

A summary for \texttt{assoc} is useless if the user can shadow it. In FOL, \texttt{fol.core} exports nothing. A user-package \texttt{assoc} might refer to a local variable or a different library's function. Our compiler resolves calls to standard library functions by checking if the function name exists in a special list (\texttt{+standard-fol-functions+}) and is not locally shadowed.

Our analysis mirrors this exactly. The Tier-1 summary table is consulted only for names resolved in the same way, using per-compilation-unit exclusions (\seqsplit{\texttt{*file-function-defs*}}) to account for local definitions. This leads to a simple but powerful guarantee:

\begin{lemma}[Name-Resolution Faithfulness]
\label{lem:nameres}
Any program for which the analysis misjudges a function's effect (by applying a summary to the wrong function) is a program where the compiler itself would have resolved the name to the same, wrong function. The analysis is no less sound than the language's own resolution model.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The emitter and the analysis gate on the same predicate: a name $n$ resolves to the standard library iff $n$ appears in \texttt{+standard-fol-functions+} and is not excluded by the compilation unit's local definitions. The analysis consults the summary table only under this predicate, and the emitter resolves the call to the summarized function under exactly the same predicate, evaluated over the same per-unit exclusion set. Hence if a summary is ever applied to a call that executes a different function, the emitter resolved that call to the different function as well---the divergence originates in the program's shadowing, not in the analysis.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Tier-2: Inferred Summaries for User Functions}
\label{sec:tier2}

Tier-1 covers the standard library, but accumulators are frequently threaded through user-defined helpers, as in \texttt{(recur (add-item acc x) ...)}. Without a summary for \texttt{add-item}, the analysis must conservatively treat the call as \texttt{:retained}. Tier-2 closes this \emph{knowledge} gap by inferring summaries for user function definitions over the same effect lattice. (It does not by itself close the \emph{conversion} gap: as §8 and RQ3 discuss, the rewriter can only rewrite operations with transient counterparts, so a linearly-threading helper is correctly understood but not yet converted through.)

When a definition is compiled, a single-pass analysis walks its body and joins an effect for each parameter: appearing as an argument to a callee with a known summary contributes that callee's per-argument effect; being returned in tail position contributes \texttt{:shared-with-result} (and marks the function as not returning a fresh value); capture by a closure or flow into a call with no summary contributes \texttt{:retained}. Calls to not-yet-summarized functions—including the function itself, and mutually recursive companions—are resolved by iterating the single-pass inference to a \emph{fixed point} over the summary cache: each round re-runs inference with the summaries produced by the previous round, until summaries stabilize. Because the lattice is finite and joins are monotone, the iteration converges; a small iteration bound guards against pathologies, and non-convergence simply leaves the function unsummarized, which is conservative (RQ3 validates self- and mutual recursion at the analysis level).

Inferred summaries are consulted by the classifier exactly as Tier-1 summaries are, and inherit name-resolution faithfulness (§3.3): they are cached under the same names the compiler resolves. Soundness under redefinition is preserved by the same mechanism as §5: the notification emitted when a definition executes both flips dependent validity cells \emph{and} evicts the redefined name's inferred summary, so stale inferences cannot outlive the code they described.

\subsection{The Classifier}

The classifier walks the loop body, assigning a usage kind to every appearance of the accumulator candidate. It is defined by a grammar of sanctioned uses. Key rules include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tail-position propagation:} Tail position is tracked through \texttt{if}, \texttt{do}, \texttt{let}, \texttt{case}, and \texttt{cond}. An accumulator in tail position is a candidate for an exit.
    \item \textbf{Literals:} An accumulator inside a literal (\texttt{[acc]}, \texttt{\#\{acc\}}) is a sanctioned exit if the literal itself is in a tail/exit position.
    \item \textbf{Recur handling:} An accumulator in a \texttt{recur} argument position is classified as \texttt{:recur-update} if it's part of a sanctioned update chain.
    \item \textbf{Reads:} An accumulator in argument position 0 of a whitelisted read-only function (e.g., \texttt{get}, \texttt{count}) is classified as \texttt{:read-ok}. Using an accumulator as a \emph{key} in a map lookup, for example, would be a disqualifier, as it relies on value semantics that mutation would violate.
    \item \textbf{Disqualifiers:} Any non-sanctioned use (e.g., passing to an unknown function, being stored in a closure) disqualifies the candidate. Two special disqualifiers ensure alignment with the rewriter:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{:recur\_reset}: The accumulator is reset to a new value not derived from the old one (e.g., \texttt{(recur \{\} ...)}). This would require changing representation mid-loop.
        \item \texttt{:recur\_in\_complex\_context}: The update expression is too complex for the rewriter to transform (e.g., \texttt{(recur (if (foo) (assoc acc k v) acc))}).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Chains}

Update chains are sequences of operations that thread the accumulator linearly. The classifier recognizes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct calls:} \texttt{(assoc acc k v)}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{->} threads:} \texttt{(-> acc (assoc k v) (dissoc k2))}
    \item \textbf{If-branched chains:} \texttt{(if test (assoc acc k v) (dissoc acc k2))} where both branches are valid chains.
    \item \textbf{Reduce init-threading:} A \texttt{reduce} form is itself a valid chain link if its accumulator is threaded linearly. For \texttt{(reduce f init-val coll)}, if \texttt{init-val} is a chain on the outer accumulator, and the reducing function \texttt{f} qualifies under special fold rules (its own accumulator is used linearly and it doesn't capture the outer one), the entire \texttt{reduce} is treated as a single \texttt{:update} link. This allows optimizing loops that contain inner reduction loops, a common pattern.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Formal Core}
\label{sec:formal}

We formalize the analysis for a core calculus with a judgment $\Gamma; \Sigma \vdash e \Downarrow U$: in context $\Gamma = (\tau, acc)$, where $\tau \in \{\mathsf{Tail}, \mathsf{NonTail}\}$ tracks whether $e$'s value flows to the loop's exit, expression $e$ has usage set $U$ given operator summaries $\Sigma$. We write $\Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}$ for $\Gamma$ with $\tau$ forced to $\mathsf{NonTail}$. Two side-condition predicates are used as black boxes, already fully defined in prose: $\mathrm{Chain}(e', acc)$ (§3.5: a direct update call, a \texttt{->} thread, an if-branched chain, or a linearly-threading \texttt{reduce}, rooted at $acc$) and $\mathrm{RefersTo}(e, acc)$ ($acc$ occurs syntactically in $e$). \texttt{let}, \texttt{case}, and \texttt{cond} desugar analogously to \textsc{if}/\textsc{do} below (tail position threads to the last or selected branch) and are omitted for space. The rules are tried in the order listed; \textsc{none} and \textsc{escape} are fallbacks that apply only when no rule above them matches, which makes the judgment total and deterministic over every subexpression of the loop body---not just the ones excerpted in earlier sections.

\begin{mathpar}\footnotesize
\inferrule*[Right=lit-exit]
 { \Gamma.\tau = \mathsf{Tail} \\ acc \in \mathrm{elements}(v) \\ \Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}; \Sigma \vdash e_j \Downarrow U_j \ (e_j \in \mathrm{elements}(v),\, e_j \neq acc) }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash v \Downarrow \{\texttt{:exit-in-literal}\} \cup \bigcup_j U_j }

\inferrule*[Right=var-exit]
 { \Gamma.\tau = \mathsf{Tail} }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash acc \Downarrow \{\texttt{:exit-bare}\} }

\inferrule*[Right=read]
 { \Sigma(f) = \text{read-only} \\ e_1 = acc \\ \Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}; \Sigma \vdash e_i \Downarrow U_i \ (i>1) }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash (f~e_1 \dots e_n) \Downarrow \{\texttt{:read-ok}\} \cup \bigcup_{i>1} U_i }

\inferrule*[Right=recur-update]
 { e' \text{ is } acc\text{'s recur-slot argument} \\ \mathrm{Chain}(e', acc) \\ \Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}; \Sigma \vdash e_i \Downarrow U_i \ (i \neq acc\text{'s slot}) }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash (\texttt{recur} \ldots e' \ldots) \Downarrow \{\texttt{:recur-update}\} \cup \bigcup_i U_i }

\inferrule*[Right=recur-reset]
 { e' \text{ is } acc\text{'s recur-slot argument} \\ \neg\,\mathrm{RefersTo}(e', acc) \\ \Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}; \Sigma \vdash e_i \Downarrow U_i \ (i \neq acc\text{'s slot}) }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash (\texttt{recur} \ldots e' \ldots) \Downarrow \{\texttt{:recur-reset}\} \cup \bigcup_i U_i }

\inferrule*[Right=recur-complex]
 { e' \text{ is } acc\text{'s recur-slot argument} \\ \mathrm{RefersTo}(e', acc) \\ \neg\,\mathrm{Chain}(e', acc) \\ \Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}; \Sigma \vdash e_i \Downarrow U_i \ (i \neq acc\text{'s slot}) }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash (\texttt{recur} \ldots e' \ldots) \Downarrow \{\texttt{:recur-in-complex-context}\} \cup \bigcup_i U_i }

\inferrule*[Right=if]
 { \Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}; \Sigma \vdash e_t \Downarrow U_t \\ \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash e_c \Downarrow U_c \\ \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash e_a \Downarrow U_a }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash (\text{if}~e_t~e_c~e_a) \Downarrow U_t \cup U_c \cup U_a }

\inferrule*[Right=do]
 { \Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}; \Sigma \vdash e_i \Downarrow U_i \ (i<n) \\ \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash e_n \Downarrow U_n }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash (\text{do}~e_1 \ldots e_n) \Downarrow \bigcup_{i \le n} U_i }

\inferrule*[Right=none]
 { \neg\,\mathrm{RefersTo}(e, acc) }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash e \Downarrow \emptyset }

\inferrule*[Right=escape]
 { \text{no rule above applies to } e }
 { \Gamma; \Sigma \vdash e \Downarrow \{\texttt{:escape}\} }
\end{mathpar}

\textsc{read} and \textsc{recur} are the only rules that inspect a specific argument position; an occurrence of $acc$ anywhere else---an argument to an unsummarized call, a closure body, a map key---falls through every rule above \textsc{escape} and is rejected by it, since \textsc{none} does not apply (the occurrence means $\mathrm{RefersTo}$ holds) and no earlier rule's more specific pattern matched. This is exactly the \texttt{:escape} case of the vocabulary below, now derived rather than asserted.

The full use vocabulary---there are no unlisted cases, and every tag below is produced by exactly one rule above---is:

\emph{Sanctioned uses} (the set \emph{SanctionedUses}):
\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{:recur-update($e'$)}] $acc$ is consumed by a recognized update chain $e'$ (a summarized update call, a \texttt{->} thread, an if-branched chain, or a linearly-threading \texttt{reduce}) whose result flows to $acc$'s position in a \texttt{recur}.
    \item[\texttt{:exit-bare}] $acc$ is returned directly, in tail position (a sanctioned exit).
    \item[\texttt{:exit-in-literal}] $acc$ is returned inside a collection literal that is itself in tail position.
    \item[\texttt{:read-ok}] $acc$ appears in argument position 0 of a whitelisted read-only operation.
\end{description}

\emph{Disqualifying uses} (any occurrence forces $\neg$\emph{Qualifies}):
\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{:recur-reset}] a \texttt{recur} rebinds $acc$ to a value not derived from it (representation would change mid-loop).
    \item[\texttt{:recur-in-complex-context}] the update expression is outside the rewriter's grammar.
    \item[\texttt{:escape}] every other occurrence: an argument to a call without a summary (or whose summary is \texttt{:retained} or \texttt{:shared-with-result} in a non-chain position), capture by a closure, storage into a data structure outside a sanctioned exit, or use as a map key or set element (which depends on value identity that mutation would violate).
\end{description}

\emph{Qualification Rule:}
\[
\frac{\Gamma \vdash \text{init fresh} \quad \forall u \in \text{uses}(\text{body}, acc),\ u \in \text{SanctionedUses}}{\text{Qualifies}(\texttt{loop [acc init] body})}
\]
where \emph{init fresh} holds when the initializer is a collection literal or a constructor call summarized as returning an unaliased root---the same root-level convention of §3.2 that licenses treating \texttt{assoc}/\texttt{conj} as returning a fresh root---and $\mathrm{uses}(\mathrm{body}, acc) = U$ such that $\Gamma_0; \Sigma \vdash \mathrm{body} \Downarrow U$ with $\Gamma_0.\tau = \mathsf{Tail}$ (the loop body's own tail position is the loop's exit point) --- i.e.\ \emph{uses} is exactly the judgment of the rules above, closing the connection between the classifier and the qualification test.

\textbf{Trusted assumptions.} The soundness results are conditional on three explicitly trusted components:
\begin{description}
    \item[(A1) Summary soundness.] Each Tier-1 summary correctly bounds the corresponding library function's effects on its arguments. Tier-1 is a hand-verified trusted base (\textasciitilde75 entries, protected by package locks); Tier-2 summaries are derived from Tier-1 by the monotone inference of §\ref{sec:tier2} and inherit its trust.
    \item[(A2) Transient-protocol correctness.] Each transient operation \texttt{op!} applied to an unaliased collection is observationally equivalent to its persistent counterpart \texttt{op} followed by discarding the input, and \texttt{persistent!}$\circ$\texttt{transient} is the identity. This is the contract of the edit-token implementation (§4.3), exercised directly by the ownership test suite.
    \item[(A3) Resolution faithfulness.] Names are resolved as modeled---which is Lemma~\ref{lem:nameres}, discharged above.
\end{description}

\begin{lemma}[Classifier Soundness]
\label{lem:classifier}
Assume A1 and A3. If \emph{Qualifies}(\texttt{loop [acc init] body}), then in every execution of the loop, at each \texttt{recur} the current accumulator value is referenced only by the loop binding itself (uniqueness-at-death), and at each exit the escaping value is referenced only by the exit expression.
\end{lemma}

\emph{Proof sketch.} By induction on iterations, with the invariant that the loop binding is the sole live reference to the accumulator value. The base case is the freshness premise. For the inductive step, consider each occurrence of $acc$ in the body---by qualification, each is a sanctioned use. A \texttt{:read-ok} use passes $acc$ to an operation whose summary (A1, applied to the function actually called by A3) guarantees the argument is neither retained nor shared with the result, so no reference survives the call. A \texttt{:recur-update} consumes $acc$ in a chain each of whose links, by its summary, returns a fresh root without retaining its input; the \texttt{recur} then rebinds the binding to the chain's result, re-establishing the invariant. Exits terminate the loop, transferring the sole reference to the exit expression. Since disqualifying uses are excluded by qualification and the use vocabulary is exhaustive over occurrences of $acc$, no alias-creating use exists. $\square$

\begin{theorem}[Soundness of Conversion]
\label{thm:sound}
Assume A1--A3. If \emph{Qualifies}(\texttt{loop [acc init] body}), then replacing the persistent operations on \texttt{acc} with their transient counterparts and inserting \texttt{persistent!} at all exits yields a program observationally equivalent to the original.
\end{theorem}

The proof proceeds by a bisimulation against a store-based semantics: Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier} supplies the uniqueness invariant the bisimulation maintains, and A2 supplies the per-step equivalence of each rewritten operation. The ownership tokens of the transient implementation (§4.3) discharge the obligations regarding interior node sharing. The detailed sketch is in Appendix A.

\section{The Transformation and the Transient Representation}
\subsection{The Rewriter}
Once the classifier qualifies a loop, the rewriter transforms the code.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initialization:} The initial binding is wrapped in a \texttt{(transient ...)} call.
    \item \textbf{Update Chains:} At each \texttt{recur} site, the update expression is transformed into its "bang" equivalent (e.g., \texttt{assoc} → \texttt{assoc!}).
    \item \textbf{Exits:} Every exit point (bare, in a chain, or in a literal) is wrapped in \texttt{(persistent! ...)}.
    \item \textbf{Modes:} The rewriter operates in \texttt{:loop} or \texttt{:reduce} mode. In \texttt{:reduce} mode, the transient is threaded through the reduction without intermediate \texttt{persistent!} boundaries, enabling full optimization of folds.
\end{enumerate}
The transformation preserves structure sharing for any subtrees that are not modified.

\subsection{Per-representation Op Gates}
Not all persistent collections have a full-featured transient equivalent. Our rewriter enforces this with \emph{op gates}. Before converting a loop, it collects the set of all update and read operations used on the accumulator. It then checks this set against the allowed operations for the accumulator's data type:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dicts:} \texttt{\{assoc!, dissoc!\}} + reads (\texttt{get}, \texttt{contains?}, etc.)
    \item \textbf{Vectors:} \texttt{\{conj!\}} + reads
    \item \textbf{Sets:} \texttt{\{conj!, disj!\}} (no reads supported)
\end{itemize}
If a loop uses an unsupported operation (e.g., trying to read from a transient set), the chain is considered incompatible, and the loop is left unconverted. This ensures at compile time that converted code can never hit an unsupported operation.

\subsection{Edit-tagged Transients}
Our implementation is based on edit-tagged transients, inspired by Clojure.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HAMT (Dicts/Sets):} Nodes in the trie are tagged with an "edit" token. When a transient is created, a new token is minted. On a write, \texttt{ensure-editable} checks the node's tag. If it matches the transient's token, the update is in-place. If not (the node is from a prior persistent structure), a copy is made, tagged, and then mutated. \texttt{persistent!} freezes the transient by clearing the token, making all its nodes read-only.
    \item \textbf{Vectors:} A transient vector has an owned, mutable tail array. \texttt{conj!} appends to this tail. If the tail overflows, a new one is allocated, and the path is updated via the same copy-on-write mechanism as HAMTs. This makes appends O(1) on average, with an O(32) cost at boundary crossings.
    \item \textbf{Transient-aware Reads:} A critical detail is that a standard persistent lookup function will \textbf{silently misread} an edited transient, as it doesn't know to check for copied-on-write nodes. Our read operations (\texttt{get}, \texttt{count}, etc.) are transient-aware: they accept both persistent and transient collections and use the correct internal lookup function (\texttt{hamt-get-transient}, \seqsplit{\texttt{\%transient\_vec\_t\_ref}}) when a transient is detected.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dynamic-extent Closures}
As a related optimization, the analysis infrastructure is also used to identify closures with dynamic extent. A literal \texttt{fn} passed to a verified non-retaining, eager higher-order function (like \texttt{map} or \texttt{reduce}) can be stack-allocated. This optimization is also world-guarded, as a redefinition of \texttt{map} to be retaining would otherwise cause a dangling pointer to a stack object.

\section{Soundness under Live Redefinition}
\textbf{Threat Model:} A converted loop bakes in the semantic assumptions from our Tier-1 summaries (e.g., that \texttt{assoc} is a pure function). A subsequent runtime definition, like \texttt{(defn assoc [c k v] (log! c) ...)} or \texttt{(defmethod assoc :around ...)} invalidates this assumption.

\textbf{Mechanism:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dual-path Emission:} The compiler emits both the optimized (fast) and original (slow) code paths for each converted region.
    \item \textbf{Region Registration:} At load time, each converted region calls \texttt{(register-region)} which returns a \emph{validity cell}. The region is registered as a dependent on every standard library function name it assumes (e.g., \texttt{assoc}, \texttt{conj}).
    \item \textbf{Invalidation:} In optimizer mode, definitions for functions like \texttt{assoc} are wrapped with a call to \seqsplit{\texttt{(note-redefinition 'assoc)}}. This function looks up all dependent validity cells for \texttt{assoc} and flips them to \texttt{invalid}.
    \item \textbf{Guard Check:} The fast path is guarded by a check on the validity cell, performed once at region entry. If invalid, execution takes the slow path.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The visibility contract.} The guard provides \emph{entry-level} consistency, and we state precisely what that does and does not promise. A region entered while its assumptions hold runs to completion under the definitions in force at entry; a redefinition becomes visible at the region's next entry. Two scenarios exercise the boundary of this contract. \emph{Concurrent redefinition:} the invalidation write is a single monotonic store to the cell, so a racing entry observes either the old or the new validity---never a torn state---and a stale read merely delays the fallback by one activation, observationally identical to the redefinition having landed slightly later. \emph{Self-inflicted redefinition:} user code invoked from inside a converted region may redefine an assumed operator mid-activation. Here the contract is a genuine semantic choice: the original late-bound program would observe the new definition at its next call, whereas the converted region completes under its entry-time definitions. We adopt this per-region snapshot semantics deliberately---it is the same visibility rule Julia's world age gives running code, where a method defined mid-execution is invisible until control returns to a newer world \cite{belyakova2020jit}. The alternative, re-checking the cell on every iteration, would buy per-iteration visibility at a per-iteration cost, for the pathological case of a loop redefining its own primitives. Under either contract the region cannot crash or corrupt state: within the region the accumulator is touched only through compiler-owned transient operations, whose behavior does not depend on the redefined name.

\textbf{Corollary (soundness of the deployed system).} Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} establishes soundness for a single compile-time snapshot of $\Sigma$; taken alone it says nothing about a running program that later redefines an assumed name. The guard mechanism above closes exactly that gap. Every activation of a converted region falls into one of two cases: (a) it enters under a valid cell, in which case Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}'s assumptions (A1--A3) held at compile time and, by the entry-snapshot visibility contract just stated, continue to hold for the entire activation, so the theorem applies unchanged; or (b) it finds the cell invalid and takes the always-sound original path, to which no soundness obligation attaches. Since these two cases are exhaustive and mutually exclusive at every entry, the deployed system---the compile-time classifier composed with the runtime guards---is observationally equivalent to the original program at every call, for any interleaving of redefinitions with region activations, modulo only the entry-snapshot granularity of case (a) already characterized above.

This mechanism provides precise, per-name dependency tracking. It differs from Julia's world age counter \cite{belyakova2020jit} in what it protects (escape/uniqueness facts rather than method dispatch tables), its granularity (region rather than method), and its invalidation mechanism (per-name dependency lists rather than a global monotonic counter), and it requires no runtime recompilation---while, as noted above, sharing world age's entry-snapshot visibility rule. A panic switch to disable all optimizations serves as a first line of defense.

In a batch compilation mode (\seqsplit{\texttt{*sealed\_world*}}), we can assume the world is closed, and no guards are emitted.

\section{Implementation}
The system is implemented in \textasciitilde2,430 lines of Common Lisp, spread across \texttt{summaries.lisp}, \texttt{escape-analysis.lisp}, \texttt{world.lisp}, and hooks in the compiler. It is covered by 220 dedicated checks across the summary, analysis, and conversion test suites; the full project suite (3,315 checks) passes at 100\%.

A key methodological tool was the \textbf{audit mode}. This mode enables the analysis to run against any codebase, collecting counts of convertible sites, disqualification reasons, and op-gate statistics, but without emitting any transformed code. We used this to size the opportunity and guide precision improvements before writing a single line of rewriter code. For example, the audit on LSim showed 17 loops with 9 accumulator candidates, all of which qualified after we implemented \texttt{reduce} init-threading and literal-exit support. The audit also showed that Tier-1 summaries covered 54.8\% of 542 call sites in LSim's hot loop, with the remainder being genuine user code.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
We evaluate our work by answering eight research questions. The RQ2 microbenchmarks were measured on an AMD Ryzen 5 7430U (single-threaded) with SBCL 2.6.0 (1\,GB dynamic space): one warm-up call, then five timed calls with a forced full collection before each, reported as mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation, with per-call allocation read from the runtime's bytes-consed counter. The ``Off'' column compiles the same source with \emph{every} optimization flag disabled (transient conversion, scalar replacement, numeric specialization, dynamic-extent closures, optimized constructors); ``On'' enables all five together, i.e.\ the pipeline a deployed build actually runs, not transient conversion in isolation. RQ7 confirms disabled emission is byte-stable relative to the pre-work compiler (the \texttt{v0.1.1} tag). We verified by inspecting emitted code which of the other four flags actually fire on these workloads, since crediting the wrong mechanism would misattribute the paper's own contribution. None define or construct record classes, so scalar replacement and optimized constructors never fire on any of them. Numeric specialization, by contrast, \emph{does} fire, and materially: every microbenchmark and the quicksort program (Table~\ref{tab:programs}) seed a loop counter from an integer literal (\texttt{i 0} or \texttt{i 1}); that counter---never the collection accumulator itself, whose type is never provable---is proven integer-typed and rewritten to unboxed arithmetic (a \texttt{(cl:type integer i)} declaration and \texttt{inc}$\to$\texttt{1+}), independently of whether the accumulator converts to a transient. Word-count is the one workload in our evaluation with no explicit counter to specialize. Dynamic-extent closures fire on the two \texttt{reduce}-based workloads (reduce, word-count), stack-allocating the literal fold function. The reported numbers should therefore be read as transient conversion's effect measured inside the full pipeline, with verified, non-trivial contributions from numeric specialization (every workload but word-count) and dynamic-extent closures (the two \texttt{reduce}-based workloads)---not as transient conversion in isolation, and not as five optimizations compounding uniformly. The RQ6 whole-program benchmarks (quicksort, word-count) were measured on the same machine and protocol. The ablation (RQ5), the LSim end-to-end figures (RQ6), and cost-when-off (RQ7) retain the original measurements from the development machine (Ryzen 9 5900X, SBCL 2.6.0, best-of-3 or mean-of-5 as noted); their allocation ratios, which are deterministic, agree with the re-measured workloads.

\textbf{RQ1: Correctness.} Our system passes the full 3,315-check project suite, including specific tests for ownership semantics (e.g., ensuring \texttt{persistent!} freezes a transient). The LSim event simulator produces byte-identical output (over full event histories of 876 and 281,248 gate evaluations) when run with the baseline and the optimized compiler.

\textbf{RQ3: Tier-2 inference on recursive functions.} The fixed-point inference of §\ref{sec:tier2} is validated at the \emph{analysis} level: the inference test suite verifies that summaries converge and are correct for a self-recursive accumulator pattern and for mutual recursion (\texttt{is-even?}/\texttt{is-odd?}). The bounded-iteration fallback is implemented (§\ref{sec:tier2}) but, we note honestly, not itself exercised by a dedicated non-convergent test case; all tested patterns converge well within the iteration bound. We report no end-to-end recursion speedups, because inferred summaries do not yet feed the \emph{conversion} client: by the alignment discipline, an update chain must consist of operations the rewriter can replace with transient counterparts, and a user-defined helper has none. §8 discusses what consuming inferred summaries would take.

Conversion removes 8.7--11$\times$ of allocation and closes 74--80\% of the wall-time gap to handwritten mutable Common Lisp: the dict loop, for instance, goes from 34$\times$ slower than a hash-table loop to 9.6$\times$; the residual is generic dispatch and boxing, which transients do not address. Two further observations. First, the transient rewrite's own output is exactly the program a Clojure programmer would write by hand with transients (§2), and the entry guard that wraps it is free (RQ7); the additional numeric-specialization win on the loop counter (§7, verified on every row but word-count) is a separate, genuine benefit of running inside a full automatic pipeline that a manual Clojure-style port would not receive unless the programmer also hand-added type declarations. The ``On'' column is therefore a fair measure of what automation delivers end-to-end, not solely a zero-cost-automation-of-transients claim. Second, even small-N loops with only 3 operations per boundary are modest wins (1.36--1.37$\times$), where wrapper transients' O(n) boundaries would have lost performance (RQ5).

\textbf{RQ4: Profitability heuristic.} Conversion cost has two distinct axes, and it is important not to conflate them. The first---\emph{operations per transient/persistent boundary}---is addressed by edit-tagged transients themselves: as Table~\ref{tab:microbenchmarks} shows, even three-update boundaries are modest wins (1.36--1.37$\times$). The second is the \emph{initial size} of an accumulator that starts from an already-populated collection: entering and leaving the transient protocol has constant costs that amortize only once enough structure is touched. Benchmarking a fixed number of updates against varying initial sizes locates the crossover at size 16 for dictionaries and 12 for vectors. For loops whose accumulator begins from a \emph{non-empty} collection, the compiler therefore ANDs the world guard with a runtime size check, \texttt{(< threshold (count acc))}, checked once at loop entry. Accumulators grown from an empty literal---the common case, and the most profitable one---are always converted: their initial count could never exceed a threshold, so guarding them on initial size would make the fast path unreachable precisely where it pays most. The measured worst cases for unguarded empty-init loops are break-even to modest wins (Table~\ref{tab:microbenchmarks}), so no iteration-count guard is emitted.

\textbf{RQ5: Ablation: wrapper vs edit-tagged transients} (original development machine). To validate our cost model, we compared our edit-tagged implementation to a hypothetical wrapper-based one (simulated by disabling reads). Edit-tagged transients improve speedups from 2.30× to 4.14× for dictionaries and 5.60× to 7.52× for vectors on large loops. For small-N loops, they turn a \textasciitilde1.0× result into a 1.16× win and enable conversion of read-heavy loops that were previously impossible (unconvertible → 1.38×). This confirms that lowering the boundary cost from O(n) to O(1)/O(32) is critical.

\textbf{RQ6: Whole-program benchmarks.} Microbenchmarks isolate the accumulation loop; a whole program dilutes the win with the surrounding work the optimization does not touch. We measured three complete programs spanning the spectrum from accumulation-dominated to accumulation-incidental (Table~\ref{tab:programs}, same machine and protocol as RQ2; LSim as noted).

\emph{Word-count} (tally 200k tokens over 997 keys into a dict, via a \texttt{reduce} that both reads and updates the accumulator each step) is accumulation-dominated: conversion yields \textbf{3.5$\times$} wall-time and \textbf{17$\times$} allocation, and the converted code runs within \textbf{3.9$\times$} of a hand-written mutable hash-table loop---the read-heavy fold that edit-tagged transients specifically enable. Unlike every other workload in this evaluation, word-count has no explicit loop counter, so numeric specialization does not apply here (§7); its literal fold function does qualify for dynamic-extent closure allocation, though that is not the source of the dict's own allocation savings. This is the clearest evidence in our evaluation that a whole program's runtime, not just an isolated loop, is materially improved by the technique.

\emph{Quicksort} (partition by building two fresh child vectors in one two-accumulator loop, then recurse) is the algorithmic tier: \textbf{1.73$\times$} wall-time and \textbf{3.3$\times$} allocation. The dilution is honest---each level also does unconverted reads, a \texttt{concat}, and recursion---and the conversion fires automatically at every level of the recursion; the partition loop's index counter is also numeric-specialized (§7), the same verified effect present in the microbenchmarks. A native destructive array sort (\texttt{cl:sort}, a different algorithm over unboxed storage) is another 16$\times$ faster, locating persistent quicksort's residual cost in representation, not just allocation.

The \emph{same} quicksort written in the in-place-swap style---threading the input vector through the loop and updating it with \texttt{assoc}---is \emph{correctly refused}: the threaded accumulator fails freshness (it is the caller's argument, not a fresh allocation), and \texttt{assoc} is not in the vector op-gate (only \texttt{conj!}). This is the alignment discipline drawing its line inside a single familiar algorithm: the profitable style converts, the inconvertible style safely does not.

\textbf{RQ7: Cost when off / inapplicable.} When the optimization flag is off, performance is identical to the v0.1.1 baseline within measurement noise: the dict-loop workload of Table~\ref{tab:microbenchmarks} measures $206\pm2$\,ms with the flag off on the current compiler versus $208\pm9$\,ms on the unmodified \texttt{v0.1.1} tag (same machine, same 5-trial protocol), and emitted code is byte-stable by test. \textbf{RQ8: Live invalidation.} We tested the invalidation mechanism by running a converted loop, redefining \texttt{assoc} to include side effects, and re-running the loop. The same function object correctly fell back to the slow path on its next entry---as the entry-level visibility contract (§5) specifies---producing identical results to the original, with world-stats counters confirming that only the \texttt{assoc}-dependent regions were invalidated.

\textbf{Applicability beyond FOL.} To estimate how often the target pattern occurs in real code, we ran a reader-level corpus analyzer (released with our artifact) over 29 open-source Clojure projects (2{,}905 files); Clojure shares FOL's persistent collections and its \texttt{loop}/\texttt{recur} and \texttt{reduce} idioms. Of 1{,}442 loop/reduce accumulator sites, 12.7\% rebuild a \emph{map} accumulator each iteration and 21.9\% grow a \emph{vector/set/list} accumulator---together about a third of all accumulator sites (34.6\%) match the shape this optimization targets. Clojure is a proxy, not a measurement of FOL, and reader-level detection undercounts (initializers built by helpers are missed), so we read these as order-of-magnitude applicability rather than precise incidence.

\textbf{Threats to Validity.} \emph{Construct:} the microbenchmarks are synthetic and isolate the accumulation pattern; the small-N rows bound the worst case rather than sample realistic mixes. \emph{Internal:} timings are wall-clock on a desktop OS; we mitigate with five runs, forced collections, and mean $\pm$ standard deviation, and the allocation ratios---which are deterministic---corroborate the timing ratios. The profitability thresholds (16/12) were measured on one machine and are conservative constants, not universal ones. \emph{External:} one language and one end-to-end application (LSim); the Clojure corpus above is a proxy for incidence; and the ablation and end-to-end figures (RQ5--RQ7) were measured on the original development machine rather than re-measured alongside RQ2.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{DVI does not convert---and why.} The DVI benchmark accumulates through a user-defined helper, \texttt{(add-item cart price)}. Tier-2 inference (§\ref{sec:tier2}) correctly determines that \texttt{add-item} threads its accumulator linearly, but the conversion client cannot yet act on that knowledge: by alignment, the rewriter only rewrites operations it can replace with transient counterparts, and it cannot reach inside user code to rewrite \texttt{add-item}'s interior \texttt{assoc}. DVI therefore remains unconverted, keeping the pathological GC behavior that helped motivate this work. Closing this gap requires either inlining qualifying helpers at the update position (the approach our companion work takes for record accumulators) or specializing transient variants of helpers; both preserve alignment, because the rewritten body then becomes visible to the op gates.

\textbf{Profitability.} Our small-N wins are modest, bounded by the constant-factor costs of the transient machinery. The profitability heuristic (RQ4) confines conversion of accumulators that start from an already-populated collection to initial sizes above the measured crossover (16 for dictionaries, 12 for vectors), while accumulators grown from an empty literal---the common and most profitable shape---are always converted. The residual exposure is an empty-init loop that performs very few updates; the measured cases of that shape are break-even to modest wins, so we accept it rather than pay an iteration-count guard on every loop entry.

\textbf{Completeness.} Our transient sets are "wrapper" style and do not support reads. Transient vectors only support \texttt{conj!}. The op-gate mechanism makes these completeness limitations, not correctness issues; the system safely refuses to convert loops that use unsupported operations.

This work represents the first three rungs of an "invalidation ladder," including inferred user-function summaries. Future work could add monotonic redefinitions (e.g., adding a method to a generic function that doesn't affect the existing dispatch) and tripwire-based recompilation.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Perceus/FBIP.} Reinking et al. \cite{reinking2021perceus} and Lorenzen \& Leijen \cite{lorenzen2023functional} introduced compile-time lifetime analysis and ownership types to enable functional-but-in-place updates. Our work achieves a similar goal but in a dynamically typed language without requiring any type system annotations from the programmer, and we additionally handle the complexities of a live, redefinable image. At the mechanism level the two systems converge more than the type-system framing suggests: Perceus's reuse analysis inserts a runtime reuse-token check so a freed cell's storage can be claimed in place by an immediately-following allocation, and our edit-tagged transients use an analogous token (the edit tag, §4.3) to decide in-place-mutate-vs-copy at each write. The difference is where the check lives: Perceus pairs a static \emph{may-reuse} analysis with a per-cell runtime token check, while we pair a static \emph{must-be-unique} proof (the classifier) with a token minted once per transient region and never rechecked per write --- trading Perceus's per-cell runtime cost for a per-region compile-time one.

\textbf{Clojure.} Our transient protocol builds on the edit-token design pioneered by Hickey in Clojure \cite{hickey2009clojure}. The key difference is that Clojure's transients are a manual, opt-in, and potentially unsafe mechanism, whereas our system is fully automatic and sound. Our work draws on the foundational literature of persistent data structures by Bagwell \cite{bagwell2001ideal} and Steindorfer \& Vinju \cite{steindorfer2015optimizing}.

\textbf{World Age.} Julia's world age mechanism \cite{belyakova2020jit} invalidates code based on changes to method tables. As noted in §5, our work differs in what it protects (uniqueness facts vs. dispatch), its granularity (region vs. method), and its invalidation mechanism (precise dependency tracking vs. a monotonic counter), avoiding the need for runtime recompilation.

\textbf{Escape Analysis.} Our work is in the lineage of escape analysis \cite{blanchet2003escape, choi1999escape, stadler2014partial}, but our client is different. Instead of stack allocation, we target uniqueness-at-death to enable mutation of persistent data structures. Unlike classic interprocedural analyses, our boundary policy relies on trusted, name-resolution-faithful summaries of a standard library, which is a more practical approach in a dynamic language where whole-program analysis is often infeasible.

\textbf{Linear and Uniqueness Types.} The concept of unique, unaliased values dates back to linear and uniqueness types \cite{wadler1990linear}. Systems like Clean and Rust use type systems to enforce these properties. Our work provides the benefits of uniqueness for a specific, common pattern without imposing any type system obligations on the programmer.

\textbf{Runtime uniqueness and the aggregate update problem.} Establishing that an aggregate is unaliased at update time long predates persistent tries: Hudak and Bloss's \emph{aggregate update problem} \cite{hudak1985aggregate} sought exactly uniqueness-at-death for functional arrays, combining abstract interpretation with run-time reference counts. Two production designs make the uniqueness check dynamic: Swift's copy-on-write collections consult the reference count at each mutation via \texttt{isKnownUniquelyReferenced} \cite{swift2023cow}, and Lean's runtime reuses cells whose reference count is one, with compiler-inserted reset/reuse pairs \cite{ullrich2019beans}. Our classifier is the static counterpart: it proves uniqueness once, at compile time, eliminating the per-operation check---at the price of conservatism, and of the world guards that a dynamic check does not need.

\textbf{Deforestation.} The "reduce init-threading" concept (§3.6) can be seen as a form of deforestation or fusion \cite{wadler1990deforestation}, where an intermediate data structure (the result of the inner reduce) is eliminated by threading the accumulator through it.

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented a system for automatically and safely converting allocation-heavy accumulation loops into in-place updates in a dynamic, persistent-first language. By combining a novel uniqueness-at-death analysis, a rewriter aligned by construction, and a lightweight guarding mechanism for soundness under live redefinition, we achieve significant performance gains on targeted workloads. Our honest cost model and negative results clearly delineate the boundaries of the technique's effectiveness, showing that edit-tagged transients are crucial for profitability. The two-tier summary design already reaches interprocedurally into user code, including recursive helpers; future work includes extending inference to multi-clause and higher-order functions, and the monotonic-redefinition and tripwire-recompilation rungs of the invalidation ladder.

\section{Proof Sketch for the Soundness of Conversion Theorem}

The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} proceeds by bisimulation. We define an operational semantics for both the original (Source) and transformed (Target) programs and show that they are observationally equivalent. Throughout we assume A1--A3 (§\ref{sec:formal}): A2 justifies each per-operation step below, and Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier} supplies the uniqueness invariant that the bisimulation relation maintains.

\subsection{Semantics}

We model program execution with a state $\langle e, \rho, \sigma \rangle$, where $e$ is the expression to evaluate, $\rho$ is an environment mapping variable names to locations, and $\sigma$ is a store mapping locations to values.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Values:} $v ::= l | n | \dots$ where $l$ is a location in the store.
    \item \textbf{Store:} $\sigma: l \mapsto v$. Collections are represented as heap-allocated objects. A persistent map is $\text{Map}(\text{root})$. A transient map is $\text{TMap}(\text{root}, \text{tok})$, where $\text{tok}$ is a unique ownership token.
    \item \textbf{Source Semantics (Persistent):} An update \texttt{(assoc m k v)} evaluates to a \emph{new} location $l'$ pointing to a new map. The store $\sigma$ is extended to $\sigma' = \sigma \cup \{l' \mapsto \text{Map}(\text{new\_root})\}$. The original map at $\rho(m)$ is unchanged.
    \item \textbf{Target Semantics (Transient):} An update \texttt{(assoc! m k v)} \emph{mutates} the map at $\rho(m)$. If the node to be changed has the correct ownership token, it is modified in-place. Otherwise, a copy is made, tagged with the token, and then mutated. The store $\sigma$ is updated to $\sigma'$ where the object at $\rho(m)$ is modified. \texttt{(persistent! m)} "freezes" the transient by creating a new persistent map and clearing the token.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Bisimulation Relation}

We define a bisimulation relation $\approx$ between a Source state $S = \langle e_S, \rho_S, \sigma_S \rangle$ and a Target state $T = \langle e_T, \rho_T, \sigma_T \rangle$. We write $S \approx T$ if:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item $e_S$ is syntactically identical to $e_T$ outside the converted loop. Inside the loop, $e_S$ and $e_T$ correspond via the transformation rules (e.g., \texttt{assoc} vs. \texttt{assoc!}).
    \item The environments $\rho_S$ and $\rho_T$ map the same variables to corresponding locations, except for the accumulator $acc$.
    \item The stores $\sigma_S$ and $\sigma_T$ are equivalent for all locations reachable from $\rho_S$ and $\rho_T$, according to a value relation $\approx_v$.
\end{enumerate}

The value relation $v_S \approx_v v_T$ holds if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $v_S$ and $v_T$ are identical scalars.
    \item $v_S$ is a persistent collection $\text{Map}(r_S)$ and $v_T$ is a persistent collection $\text{Map}(r_T)$, and they are structurally identical.
    \item $v_S$ is the persistent accumulator $\text{Map}(r_S)$ at location $l_{acc\_S}$, and $v_T$ is the corresponding transient accumulator $\text{TMap}(r_T, \text{tok})$ at $l_{acc\_T}$, and they are structurally identical (ignoring the token).
\end{itemize}

A key part of the invariant is that for the accumulator $acc$, $\rho_S(acc)$ must be the \emph{only} reference to the map at that location in the source store. This is guaranteed by the uniqueness-at-death analysis.

\subsection{Proof Sketch by Induction on Evaluation Steps}

We show that if $S \approx T$ and the Source program takes a step $S \rightarrow S'$, then the Target program can take a step $T \rightarrow T'$ such that $S' \approx T'$.

\textbf{Base Case: Loop Initialization.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Source: \texttt{(loop [acc (hash-map)] ...)} evaluates to a state where $\rho_S(acc)$ points to a fresh, unaliased map $m_S$.
    \item Target: \texttt{(loop [acc (transient (hash-map))] ...)} evaluates to a state where $\rho_T(acc)$ points to a fresh transient map $m_T$ with a new token.
    \item The states are related: $m_S \approx_v m_T$, and the uniqueness property for $acc$ holds in the Source.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Inductive Step: Inside the Loop.} We consider the evaluation of the loop body.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Case 1: Read operation \texttt{(get acc k)}.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Source: Reads from the persistent map at $\rho_S(acc)$.
        \item Target: Reads from the transient map at $\rho_T(acc)$. Our transient-aware read operations are designed to correctly interpret the mix of original and copied-on-write nodes.
        \item Since the structures are equivalent, they return equivalent values. The resulting states $S'$ and $T'$ are still in relation.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Case 2: Update operation \texttt{(recur (assoc acc k v))}.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Source: \texttt{(assoc acc k v)} evaluates to a \emph{new} location $l_S'$ with a new map $m_S'$. The old map at $\rho_S(acc)$ is now garbage (by the last-use property from our analysis). The \texttt{recur} rebinds $acc$ to $l_S'$. The uniqueness property is maintained for the new accumulator value.
        \item Target: \texttt{(assoc! acc k v)} \emph{mutates} the transient map at $\rho_T(acc)$ in-place. The \texttt{recur} rebinds $acc$ to the same location $\rho_T(acc)$.
        \item The new map $m_S'$ and the mutated map at $\rho_T(acc)$ are structurally equivalent. The resulting states $S'$ and $T'$ are in relation, and the uniqueness invariant is maintained.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Case 3: Other uses of \texttt{acc}.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item No other use forms occur. The bisimulation is defined only for programs satisfying \emph{Qualifies}, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier} every occurrence of $acc$ in such a program is one of the sanctioned forms covered by Cases 1--2 or a loop exit. The burden of excluding alias-creating uses is therefore discharged by Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier}, not assumed here: were an unsanctioned use such as \texttt{(some-func acc)} with an unknown \texttt{some-func} present, the loop would not qualify and no transformation would occur, so no proof obligation arises.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Inductive Step: Loop Exit.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Source: The loop exits, returning the final persistent accumulator $m_S$.
    \item Target: The loop exits. The expression is wrapped with \texttt{(persistent! acc)}, which evaluates the transient $m_T$ to a new persistent map $m_T'$.
    \item Since $m_S \approx_v m_T$ before the exit, the final results $m_S$ and $m_T'$ are structurally identical and thus observationally equivalent to the rest of the program.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Conclusion.} By induction, for any number of evaluation steps, the Source and Target programs remain in the bisimulation relation. Since the initial states are related and the relation preserves program termination and final values, the transformed program is observationally equivalent to the original. The soundness of the transformation thus rests on the guarantees provided by the uniqueness-at-death classifier.
