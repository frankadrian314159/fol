\begin{abstract}
Persistent-first languages pay a large allocation tax on accumulation loops. Languages like Koka eliminate it with ownership types (Perceus/FBIP); Clojure asks the programmer to opt in manually with transients. We show the optimization can be made \emph{automatic and sound} in a latently-typed language with open-world CLOS dispatch and live redefinition—with no type system—via (1) a tail-position-sensitive \emph{usage classification} that establishes uniqueness-at-death of loop and fold accumulators, (2) a rewriter aligned with the classifier \emph{by construction}, targeting edit-tagged in-place transients, and (3) \emph{world-guarded regions} that keep the optimization sound when definitions change at runtime. The result: 3.7–4.3× wall-time and 8.7–11× allocation improvements on accumulation-bound loops—closing 75–79\% of the gap to handwritten mutable Common Lisp—1.7–3.5× on whole programs such as word-frequency counting, byte-identical outputs on a nontrivial event simulator, zero measured cost when disabled or inapplicable, and an honest cost model of when transient conversion pays.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Languages using persistent data structures offer immutability and thread-safety, but pay a performance penalty known as the "persistence tax": every update allocates a new version from the root, turning accumulation loops into excessive allocation and GC pressure.

In FOL (a Lisp dialect with persistent data structures), the cost is stark: simple accumulation loops run up to 114× slower than handwritten, mutable Common Lisp (55× more memory). The cost worsens for loops threading an accumulator through user-defined helpers; our Derived Value Invalidation (DVI) benchmark follows this pattern: 20× slower, \textbf{900×} higher in memory, from O(N²) GC pressure. This paper presents a sound, automatic optimization eliminating the tax for a large class of \emph{intraprocedural} accumulation loops; we use interprocedural DVI not as solved, but as a boundary condition delineating scope and motivating future work.

The pattern suffering this tax is the accumulation loop, common to both \texttt{loop/recur} and \texttt{reduce}:

\begin{lstlisting}
;; Loop-based accumulation
(loop [acc {}]
  ...
  (recur (assoc acc k v)))

;; Fold-based accumulation
(reduce (fn [acc x] (conj acc ...))
        [] coll)
\end{lstlisting}

Each iteration's call to \texttt{assoc} or \texttt{conj} allocates a new map or vector, discarding the old one. Key insight: if the old \texttt{acc} produces only the new \texttt{acc}---i.e., unique at its point of death---the update can happen in-place.

Existing solutions do not transfer to FOL. Perceus/FBIP \cite{reinking2021perceus} and its relatives \cite{lorenzen2023functional} rely on ownership and uniqueness types, absent here. Clojure \cite{hickey2009clojure} offers a manual, unsafe escape hatch: via \texttt{transient} structures and \texttt{assoc!}-style updates, where misuse corrupts data. FOL supports live redefinition, so assumptions baked into an optimized loop may be invalidated \emph{while it runs}.

This paper presents a fully automatic, sound system converting persistent accumulation loops to in-place transients. Contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Uniqueness-at-death by usage classification.} A flow- and tail-position-sensitive classification of accumulator uses (\texttt{:recur-update}, \texttt{:exit-bare}, etc.) establishing when it may convert in-place---without ownership or linear types---including \texttt{->} threads, if-branches, and \emph{reduce init-threading}.
    \item \textbf{Alignment-by-construction between analysis and transformation.} The classifier refuses anything the rewriter cannot reach (\texttt{:recur\_in\_complex\_context}) or changes representation mid-loop (\texttt{:recur\_reset}); per-representation \emph{op gates} are classification-time conditions, not runtime safety nets, so converted code never hits an unsupported operation.
    \item \textbf{Name-resolution-faithful, two-tier summaries.} Tier-1 matches operators exactly as the compiler resolves them (by name)---the analysis is no less sound than the language's resolution model. Tier-2 extends coverage to user functions by \emph{inferring} summaries over the same lattice via a bounded fixed point; summaries are invalidated on redefinition (§\ref{sec:tier2}).
    \item \textbf{Soundness under live redefinition without world-age infrastructure.} Converted regions compile to guarded dual paths; validity cells register at load time; definitions notify at execution time, invalidating their dependents. Guards measure as free.
    \item \textbf{An honest cost model} quantifying when conversion is profitable. Wrapper transients (O(n) boundaries) win at large ops-per-boundary; our edit-tagged transients (O(1)/O(32)) extend the win to small-N accumulations and read-heavy folds. We validate this with benchmarks, including DVI, whose accumulation flows through a helper beyond the client's reach (§\ref{sec:discussion}), and an end-to-end system bounded by factors this technique doesn't address.
\end{enumerate}

Our contribution is not a new escape analysis, but the \emph{assembly} of these techniques, solving one problem: automatic, sound transient conversion in a \emph{dynamic, open-world, JIT-less} language. Our solutions (name-resolution-faithful summaries, world-guards) are distinct from static-language (closed-world, typed) and JIT-based approaches (infrastructure FOL lacks).

Our evaluation shows 3.7--4.3× wall-time and 8.7--11× allocation on accumulation-bound microbenchmarks, closing 75--79\% of the gap to handwritten mutable Common Lisp; 1.7--3.5× on whole programs (quicksort, word-count), with the inconvertible quicksort style refused; byte-identical results on an event simulator; negligible overhead when disabled or inapplicable (§\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{Background and a Motivating Walk-through}
\label{sec:background}

\textbf{FOL in one column.} FOL is a Lisp-1 dialect transpiled to Common Lisp, with persistent data structures. Vectors are 32-way tries with a mutable tail for appends, similar to Clojure's; dictionaries are Hash Array Mapped Tries (HAMTs) \cite{bagwell2001ideal}. \texttt{(loop [bindings] ... (recur new-vals))} provides tail-call-optimized looping; \texttt{->} threads an expression through a series of forms; \texttt{reduce} applies a function to an accumulator and successive collection items. The compiler transpiles forms, resolving standard-library calls by name at emit time against a list---load-bearing for our analysis (§\ref{sec:nameres}).

\textbf{Worked Example.} \texttt{lh-pop-batch}, from the LSim simulator, builds a priority queue \texttt{acc} by \texttt{conj}'ing items, returning it in a tuple literal \texttt{[acc q]}.

\emph{Original Code:}
\begin{lstlisting}
(defn lh-pop-batch [q n]
  (loop [acc [] i 0]
    (if (< i n)
      (let [[v new-q] (pop q)]
        (recur (conj acc v) (inc i)))
      [acc q]))) ; Exit with acc in a literal
\end{lstlisting}
Each \texttt{conj} call creates a new persistent vector, copying the root path. Our analysis identifies \texttt{acc} as a candidate: freshly allocated (\texttt{[]}), never escaping the loop, used in the \texttt{recur} call and exit literal (both sanctioned).

\emph{Converted Code (Simplified):}
\begin{lstlisting}
(defn lh-pop-batch [q n]
  (if (is-valid? *lh-pop-batch-region-1*)
    ;; Fast path
    (loop [acc-t (transient []) i 0]
      (if (< i n)
        (let [[v new-q] (pop q)]
          (recur (conj! acc-t v) (inc i)))
        [(persistent! acc-t) q]))
    ;; Slow/fallback path
    (loop [acc [] i 0]
      ... ; Original code
      )))
\end{lstlisting}
The compiler emits a dual-path version: the fast path initializes a \texttt{transient} vector, updates via \texttt{conj!}, and at exit \texttt{persistent!} converts to a persistent vector in O(1) time. A guard, \texttt{is-valid?}, ensures safety---if \texttt{conj} were redefined, execution uses the original path.

\textbf{Clojure Transient Recap.} Clojure's transients provide a mutable view over a collection; updates apply in-place. Reads are problematic: a transient read might not see an in-place update. "Wrapper" transients forbid reads; "edit-tagged" transients, which we use, support reads too (§\ref{sec:edittagged}), suiting more algorithms, e.g.\ read-heavy folds.

\section{The Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

Our goal is to prove \emph{uniqueness-at-death}: the accumulator's value at each iteration produces only the next iteration's value, and the final value shares its identity with no other live object---a stronger property than escape analysis, which determines if a value \emph{may} escape a region.

\subsection{Two Properties: Escape vs. Uniqueness-at-Death}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Escape (May-analysis):} An object \emph{escapes} if its lifetime exceeds its static scope---tracked by escape analysis to enable stack allocation.
    \item \textbf{Uniqueness-at-Death (Must-analysis):} An object is \emph{unique at death} if no aliases exist at its last use. This requires:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Freshness:} The initial value is a fresh, unaliased allocation.
        \item \textbf{No-aliasing:} No new aliases outlive the iteration (e.g., storing it in a global or captured variable).
        \item \textbf{Last-use:} The \texttt{recur} or \texttt{reduce} update is the value's final use in the iteration.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

If these hold, the object's identity is not observable, and in-place mutation is safe.

\subsection{Tier-1 Summaries: The Effect Lattice}
\label{sec:tier1}

To analyze functions, we use a summary-based analysis: Tier-1 summaries cover the standard library, classifying each function's effect on its arguments, particularly the accumulator, over an effect lattice:

\texttt{:none} $<$ \texttt{:invoked} $<$ \texttt{:shared-with-result} $<$ \texttt{:retained}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{:none}: The argument is not used.
    \item \texttt{:invoked}: The argument is called as a function but not retained.
    \item \texttt{:shared-with-result}: The argument may appear in the return value (e.g., \texttt{(identity acc)}).
    \item \texttt{:retained}: The argument escapes to a persistent location (e.g., \texttt{(add-watch! acc ...)})---a disqualifier.
\end{itemize}

We hand-verified ~75 core-function summaries. The key insight, \textbf{root-level convention}: persistent operations like \texttt{assoc} and \texttt{conj} return a fresh root that may reference unchanged children. Since our transient protocol is copy-on-write and never mutates elements, this holds for all clients, including transients.

\subsection{Name-Resolution Faithfulness}
\label{sec:nameres}

A summary for \texttt{assoc} is useless if shadowable: \texttt{fol.core} exports nothing, so a user-package \texttt{assoc} might be a local variable or a different library's function. Our compiler resolves standard-library calls by checking whether the name exists in a list (\texttt{+standard-fol-functions+}) and isn't locally shadowed.

Our analysis mirrors this: the Tier-1 table is consulted only for names resolved the same way, using per-compilation-unit exclusions (\seqsplit{\texttt{*file-function-defs*}}) for local definitions, yielding a powerful guarantee:

\begin{lemma}[Name-Resolution Faithfulness]
\label{lem:nameres}
Any program for which the analysis misjudges a function's effect (by applying a summary to the wrong function) is a program where the compiler itself would have resolved the name to the same, wrong function. The analysis is no less sound than the language's own resolution model.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The emitter and the analysis gate on the same predicate: a name $n$ resolves to the standard library iff $n$ appears in \texttt{+standard-fol-functions+} and is not excluded by the compilation unit's local definitions. The analysis consults the summary table only under this predicate, and the emitter resolves the call under the same predicate and exclusions. Hence if a summary is applied to a call executing a different function, the emitter resolved that call to the same different function---the divergence originates in the program's shadowing, not the analysis.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Tier-2: Inferred Summaries for User Functions}
\label{sec:tier2}

Tier-1 covers the standard library, but accumulators thread through user-defined helpers, as in \texttt{(recur (add-item acc x) ...)}. Without a summary for \texttt{add-item}, the analysis must treat the call as \texttt{:retained}. Tier-2 closes this \emph{knowledge} gap by inferring summaries for user functions over the same lattice. (It does not close the \emph{conversion} gap: as §\ref{sec:discussion} and RQ3 discuss, the rewriter rewrites operations with transient counterparts, so a linearly-threading helper is understood but not yet converted.)

When a definition is compiled, a single-pass analysis walks its body, joining an effect for each parameter: an argument to a callee with a known summary contributes its per-argument effect; a tail-position return contributes \texttt{:shared-with-result} (marking it as not returning fresh); capture by a closure or flow into an unsummarized call contributes \texttt{:retained}. Calls to not-yet-summarized functions---including itself and mutually recursive companions---are resolved by iterating this inference to a \emph{fixed point}: each round re-runs with the previous summaries until stable. Since the lattice is finite and joins are monotone, the iteration converges; a small bound guards against pathologies; non-convergence leaves the function unsummarized, conservatively (RQ3 validates self- and mutual recursion).

Inferred summaries are consulted by the classifier as Tier-1 summaries are, inheriting name-resolution faithfulness (§\ref{sec:nameres}): they are cached under the names the compiler resolves. Soundness under redefinition is preserved by the same mechanism as §\ref{sec:soundness}: the notification emitted when a definition executes both flips dependent validity cells \emph{and} evicts the redefined name's inferred summary, so stale inferences cannot outlive their code.

\subsection{The Classifier}

The classifier walks the loop body, assigning a usage kind to every accumulator-candidate appearance, via sanctioned uses. Key rules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tail-position propagation:} Tracked through \texttt{if}, \texttt{do}, \texttt{let}, \texttt{case}, and \texttt{cond}; a tail-position accumulator is exit-eligible.
    \item \textbf{Literals:} An accumulator inside a literal (\texttt{[acc]}, \texttt{\#\{acc\}}) is a sanctioned exit if the literal is in tail/exit position.
    \item \textbf{Recur handling:} An accumulator in a \texttt{recur} argument position is \texttt{:recur-update} if part of a sanctioned chain.
    \item \textbf{Reads:} An accumulator in argument position 0 of a whitelisted read-only function (e.g., \texttt{get}, \texttt{count}) is classified as \texttt{:read-ok}. Using it as a \emph{key} in a map lookup would be a disqualifier, since mutation would violate value semantics.
    \item \textbf{Disqualifiers:} Any non-sanctioned use (e.g., passing to an unknown function, storage in a closure) disqualifies the candidate. Two disqualifiers ensure alignment with the rewriter:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{:recur\_reset}: The accumulator is reset to a value not derived from the old one (e.g., \texttt{(recur \{\} ...)}), requiring a representation change mid-loop.
        \item \texttt{:recur\_in\_complex\_context}: The update expression is too complex for the rewriter to transform (e.g., \texttt{(recur (if (foo) (assoc acc k v) acc))}).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Chains}
\label{sec:chains}

Update chains are sequences of operations threading the accumulator linearly. The classifier recognizes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct calls:} \texttt{(assoc acc k v)}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{->} threads:} \texttt{(-> acc (assoc k v) (dissoc k2))}
    \item \textbf{If-branched chains:} \texttt{(if test (assoc acc k v) (dissoc acc k2))} where branches are valid chains.
    \item \textbf{Reduce init-threading:} A \texttt{reduce} form is a valid chain link if its accumulator threads linearly. For \texttt{(reduce f init-val coll)}, if \texttt{init-val} is a chain on the outer accumulator and \texttt{f} qualifies under fold rules (its accumulator is used linearly and doesn't capture the outer one), the \texttt{reduce} is treated as a single \texttt{:update} link---optimizing loops containing inner reductions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Formal Core}
\label{sec:formal}

We formalize the analysis for a core calculus with judgment $\Gamma; \Sigma \vdash e \Downarrow U$: in context $\Gamma = (\tau, acc)$, where $\tau \in \{\mathsf{Tail}, \mathsf{NonTail}\}$ tracks whether $e$'s value flows to the loop's exit, $e$ has usage set $U$ given operator summaries $\Sigma$. $\Gamma_{\mathsf{NT}}$ is $\Gamma$ with $\tau$ forced to $\mathsf{NonTail}$. Three side-condition predicates are used as black boxes, already defined in prose: $\mathrm{Chain}(e', acc)$ (§\ref{sec:chains}, rooted at $acc$), $\mathrm{RefersTo}(e, acc)$ ($acc$ occurs syntactically in $e$), and $\mathrm{OpGate}(e', acc)$ (§\ref{sec:opgates}: every update operation in chain $e'$ has a transient, ``bang'' counterpart among $acc$'s admitted operations---fixed once, at the freshness check that licensed $acc$'s initializer). $\mathrm{Chain}$ is syntactic pattern recognition; $\mathrm{OpGate}$ is the representation-specific check that a recognized chain is executable by the rewriter, making \textsc{recur-update} below match only chains the rewriter can carry out, not merely parse. \texttt{let}, \texttt{case}, and \texttt{cond} desugar analogously to \textsc{if}/\textsc{do} below (tail position threads to the last or selected branch), omitted for space. The rules are tried in order (Figure~\ref{fig:formal-rules}); \textsc{none} and \textsc{escape} are fallbacks applying when no rule above matches, making the judgment total and deterministic over every subexpression of the loop body.

\textsc{read} and \textsc{recur} are the only rules inspecting a specific argument position; an occurrence of $acc$ anywhere else---an argument to an unsummarized call, a closure body, a map key---falls through every rule above \textsc{escape} and is rejected, since \textsc{none} does not apply ($\mathrm{RefersTo}$ holds) and no earlier rule's pattern matched. This is the \texttt{:escape} case of the vocabulary below, now derived rather than asserted.

The full use vocabulary---there are no unlisted cases, and every tag below is produced by one rule above---is:

\emph{Sanctioned uses} (the set \emph{SanctionedUses}):
\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{:recur-update($e'$)}] $acc$ is consumed by a recognized update chain $e'$ (§\ref{sec:chains}) whose result flows to $acc$'s \texttt{recur} position, with every operation in $e'$ executable per $\mathrm{OpGate}(e', acc)$.
    \item[\texttt{:exit-bare}] $acc$ is returned directly, in tail position (a sanctioned exit).
    \item[\texttt{:exit-in-literal}] $acc$ is returned inside a collection literal itself in tail position.
    \item[\texttt{:read-ok}] $acc$ appears in argument position 0 of a whitelisted read-only operation.
\end{description}

\emph{Disqualifying uses} (any occurrence forces $\neg$\emph{Qualifies}):
\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{:recur-reset}] a \texttt{recur} rebinds $acc$ to a value not derived from it (representation would change mid-loop).
    \item[\texttt{:recur-in-complex-context}] the update expression is outside the rewriter's grammar, or is a recognized chain using an operation with no transient counterpart in its op gate (§\ref{sec:opgates})---e.g.\ \texttt{assoc} on a vector, whose gate admits only \texttt{conj!}.
    \item[\texttt{:escape}] otherwise: an argument to a call without a summary (or whose summary is \texttt{:retained} or \texttt{:shared-with-result} in a non-chain position), closure capture, storage into a structure outside a sanctioned exit, or use as a map key/set element (value identity mutation would violate).
\end{description}

\emph{Qualification Rule:}
\[
\frac{\Gamma \vdash \text{init fresh} \quad \forall u \in \text{uses}(\text{body}, acc),\ u \in \text{SanctionedUses}}{\text{Qualifies}(\texttt{loop [acc init] body})}
\]
where \emph{init fresh} holds when the initializer is a collection literal or a constructor call summarized as returning an unaliased root---the same root-level convention of §\ref{sec:tier1} licensing \texttt{assoc}/\texttt{conj}'s fresh-root return---and $\mathrm{uses}(\mathrm{body}, acc) = U$ such that $\Gamma_0; \Sigma \vdash \mathrm{body} \Downarrow U$ with $\Gamma_0.\tau = \mathsf{Tail}$ (the loop body's tail position is the loop's exit point)---i.e.\ \emph{uses} is the judgment above, closing the connection between classifier and qualification test.

\textbf{Trusted assumptions.} The soundness results are conditional on three explicitly trusted components:
\begin{description}
    \item[(A1) Summary soundness.] Each Tier-1 summary correctly bounds its library function's effects on its arguments. Tier-1 is a hand-verified trusted base (\textasciitilde75 entries); the summary table is protected by package locks against accidental modification. This is independent of whether the \emph{library functions the table describes} (\texttt{assoc}, \texttt{conj}, etc.) are redefined at runtime---they can be, the threat §\ref{sec:soundness} defends against. Tier-2 summaries derive from Tier-1 via the monotone inference of §\ref{sec:tier2} and inherit its trust.
    \item[(A2) Transient-protocol correctness.] Each transient operation \texttt{op!} on an unaliased collection is observationally equivalent to \texttt{op} followed by discarding the input, and \texttt{persistent!}$\circ$\texttt{transient} is the identity---the contract of the edit-token implementation (§\ref{sec:edittagged}), exercised by the ownership test suite. This reduces to one token invariant: each live transient region mints a token unique among all live transients, so a node's tag identifies at most one owning region, and \texttt{ensure-editable}'s copy-on-mismatch rule (§\ref{sec:edittagged}) never lets two live regions mutate the same interior node. Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier} supplies uniqueness of the accumulator's \emph{root}; A2's token invariant extends that guarantee to the shared interior nodes a persistent structure's root may reference.
    \item[(A3) Resolution faithfulness.] Names are resolved as modeled---which is Lemma~\ref{lem:nameres}, discharged above.
\end{description}

\begin{lemma}[Classifier Soundness]
\label{lem:classifier}
Assume A1 and A3. If \emph{Qualifies}(\texttt{loop [acc init] body}), then in every execution of the loop, at each \texttt{recur} the current accumulator value is referenced only by the loop binding itself (uniqueness-at-death), and at each exit the escaping value is referenced only by the exit expression.
\end{lemma}

\emph{Proof sketch.} By induction on iterations, with the invariant that the loop binding is the accumulator's sole live reference. The base case is the freshness premise. For the inductive step, consider each occurrence of $acc$ in the body---by qualification, each is a sanctioned use. A \texttt{:read-ok} use passes $acc$ to an operation whose summary (A1, applied to the function A3 calls) guarantees the argument is neither retained nor shared with the result, so no reference survives the call. A \texttt{:recur-update} consumes $acc$ in a chain whose every link, by its summary, returns a fresh root without retaining its input; \texttt{recur} rebinds the binding to the chain's result, re-establishing the invariant. Exits terminate the loop, transferring the sole reference to the exit expression. Since disqualifying uses are excluded by qualification and the vocabulary is exhaustive over $acc$'s occurrences, no alias-creating use exists. $\square$

\begin{theorem}[Soundness of Conversion]
\label{thm:sound}
Assume A1--A3. If \emph{Qualifies}(\texttt{loop [acc init] body}), then replacing the persistent operations on \texttt{acc} with their transient counterparts and inserting \texttt{persistent!} at all exits yields a program observationally equivalent to the original.
\end{theorem}

The proof proceeds by a bisimulation: Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier} supplies the uniqueness invariant the bisimulation maintains, and A2 supplies the per-step equivalence of each rewritten operation. The theorem's conclusion presupposes ``their transient counterparts'' is well-defined, i.e.\ every persistent operation \emph{Qualifies} admits on \texttt{acc} has one; this is what the $\mathrm{OpGate}$ premise of \textsc{recur-update} (§\ref{sec:formal}) guarantees, by construction, for every \emph{Qualifies}-accepted occurrence---so the replacement is total over the qualifying program, not merely where available. The ownership tokens of the transient implementation (§\ref{sec:edittagged}) discharge the interior-node-sharing obligations; the detailed sketch is in Appendix A.

\section{The Transformation and the Transient Representation}
\label{sec:transformation}
\subsection{The Rewriter}
Once the classifier qualifies a loop, the rewriter transforms it.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initialization:} The binding is wrapped in a \texttt{(transient ...)} call.
    \item \textbf{Update Chains:} Each \texttt{recur} site's update becomes its "bang" equivalent (e.g., \texttt{assoc} → \texttt{assoc!}).
    \item \textbf{Exits:} Every exit (bare, in a chain, or in a literal) is wrapped in \texttt{(persistent! ...)}.
    \item \textbf{Modes:} The rewriter operates in \texttt{:loop} or \texttt{:reduce} mode; in the latter, the transient threads through without persistent! boundaries, optimizing folds.
\end{enumerate}
The transformation preserves structure sharing for unmodified subtrees.

\subsection{Per-representation Op Gates}
\label{sec:opgates}
Not all persistent collections have full-featured transient equivalents. Our rewriter enforces this with \emph{op gates}: before converting, it checks the accumulator's update/read operations against those allowed for its type:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dicts:} \texttt{\{assoc!, dissoc!\}} + reads (\texttt{get}, \texttt{contains?}, etc.)
    \item \textbf{Vectors:} \texttt{\{conj!\}} + reads
    \item \textbf{Sets:} \texttt{\{conj!, disj!\}} (no reads supported)
\end{itemize}
If a loop uses an unsupported operation (e.g., reading a transient set), the chain is incompatible, leaving the loop unconverted. This is not merely a rewriter-side safety net: it is the $\mathrm{OpGate}(e', acc)$ predicate \textsc{recur-update} requires (§\ref{sec:formal}), so a chain using an operation absent from them is never classified \texttt{:recur-update}---it falls to \texttt{:recur-in-complex-context} and disqualifies, the same alignment-by-construction discipline governing \texttt{:recur\_reset}. This ensures, at compile time, that converted code never hits an unsupported operation.

\subsection{Edit-tagged Transients}
\label{sec:edittagged}
Our implementation uses edit-tagged transients, inspired by Clojure.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HAMT (Dicts/Sets):} Trie nodes are tagged with an "edit" token; creating a transient mints a new one. On a write, \texttt{ensure-editable} checks the tag: if it matches, the update is in-place; otherwise (from a prior structure), it's copied, tagged, and mutated. \texttt{persistent!} freezes the transient by clearing the token, making nodes read-only.
    \item \textbf{Vectors:} A transient vector has an owned, mutable tail array; \texttt{conj!} appends to it. If the tail overflows, a new one is allocated and the path updated via HAMT copy-on-write, giving O(1) appends, with O(32) cost at boundary crossings.
    \item \textbf{Transient-aware Reads:} A persistent lookup function will \textbf{silently misread} an edited transient, since it doesn't check for copied-on-write nodes. Our read operations (\texttt{get}, \texttt{count}, etc.) are transient-aware: they accept both collections and dispatch to the correct lookup function (\texttt{hamt-get-transient}, \seqsplit{\texttt{\%transient\_vec\_t\_ref}}) when detected.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dynamic-extent Closures}
As an optimization, the infrastructure identifies closures with dynamic extent: a literal \texttt{fn} passed to a verified non-retaining, higher-order function (\texttt{map}, \texttt{reduce}) can be stack-allocated. This is world-guarded: redefining \texttt{map} to be retaining would leave a dangling stack-object pointer.

\section{Soundness under Live Redefinition}
\label{sec:soundness}
\textbf{Threat Model:} A converted loop bakes in semantic assumptions from Tier-1 summaries (e.g., that \texttt{assoc} is pure). A subsequent runtime definition---\texttt{(defn assoc [c k v] (log! c) ...)} or \texttt{(defmethod assoc :around ...)}---invalidates this.

\textbf{Mechanism:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dual-path Emission:} The compiler emits both optimized (fast) and original (slow) paths for each converted region.
    \item \textbf{Region Registration:} At load time, each converted region calls \texttt{(register-region)}, returning a \emph{validity cell}, and registers as dependent on every standard-library name it assumes (e.g., \texttt{assoc}, \texttt{conj}).
    \item \textbf{Invalidation:} In optimizer mode, definitions for functions like \texttt{assoc} are wrapped with a call to \seqsplit{\texttt{(note-redefinition 'assoc)}}, flipping dependent validity cells for \texttt{assoc} to \texttt{invalid}.
    \item \textbf{Guard Check:} The fast path is guarded by a validity-cell check, performed once at entry; if invalid, execution takes the slow path.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The visibility contract.} The guard provides \emph{entry-level} consistency. A region entered while its assumptions hold runs to completion under definitions fixed at entry; a redefinition becomes visible only at its next entry. \emph{Concurrent redefinition:} the invalidation write is a monotonic store to the cell, so a racing entry observes the old or new validity---never torn---and a stale read delays the fallback by one activation, as if the redefinition landed later. \emph{Self-inflicted redefinition:} user code invoked inside a converted region may redefine an operator mid-activation. This is a semantic choice: the late-bound program would observe the new definition at its next call; the converted region completes under its entry-time definitions. We adopt this per-region snapshot semantics---the same visibility rule Julia's world age gives, where a method defined mid-execution is invisible until control returns to a newer world \cite{belyakova2020jit}. Alternatively, re-checking the cell every iteration buys per-iteration visibility at a cost, for a loop redefining its primitives. Under either contract the region cannot crash or corrupt state: the accumulator is touched only through compiler-owned transient operations, whose behavior doesn't depend on the redefined name.

\textbf{Why the mutation itself needs no separate synchronization.} The guard protects against a redefinition changing what a converted region \emph{means}; it is a separate question whether in-place mutation is safe if FOL programs run multiple threads. It is, for free: uniqueness-at-death (Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier}) establishes that no reference to the accumulator survives outside the loop's binding---not merely that no \emph{same-thread} reference survives. A second thread touching the same object would be a reference the classifier's freshness and no-aliasing premises rule out, so the property licensing in-place mutation would make concurrent access impossible. No additional locking is needed.

\textbf{Corollary (soundness of the deployed system).} Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} establishes soundness for a compile-time snapshot of $\Sigma$; alone it says nothing about a program that later redefines an assumed name. The guard mechanism above closes that gap: every activation falls into two cases---(a) it enters under a valid cell, so Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}'s assumptions (A1--A3) held at compile time and, by the entry-snapshot visibility contract, continue to hold for the activation, so the theorem applies; or (b) it finds the cell invalid and takes the always-sound original path, incurring no obligation. These cases are exhaustive and mutually exclusive at every entry, so the deployed system---classifier plus guards---is observationally equivalent to the original program at every call, for any redefinition/region-activation interleaving, modulo the entry-snapshot granularity of case (a).

This mechanism provides per-name dependency tracking requiring no runtime recompilation---differing from Julia's world age counter \cite{belyakova2020jit} in ways detailed in §\ref{sec:related}, though it shares its entry-snapshot visibility rule. A panic switch disabling all optimizations serves as a first line of defense.

In batch compilation mode (\seqsplit{\texttt{*sealed\_world*}}), the world can be assumed closed, and no guards are emitted.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
The system is implemented in \textasciitilde2,430 lines of Common Lisp, across \texttt{summaries.lisp}, \texttt{escape-analysis.lisp}, \texttt{world.lisp}, and compiler hooks, covered by 220 checks across the summary, analysis, and conversion suites; the project suite (3,315 checks) passes at 100\%.

A key tool was \textbf{audit mode}, running the analysis against any codebase, collecting convertible-site counts, disqualification reasons, and op-gate statistics, without emitting transformed code. We used this to size the opportunity and guide precision improvements before writing rewriter code. The audit on LSim showed 17 loops with 9 accumulator candidates, qualifying after implementing \texttt{reduce} init-threading and literal-exit support, and that Tier-1 summaries covered 54.8\% of 542 call sites in LSim's hot loop, the remainder being user code.

\textbf{Compile-time overhead.} The analysis is a single-pass, per-form cost, not a whole-program fixed point (Tier-2's, bounded per function, §\ref{sec:tier2}), so we expect it cheap where it multiplies the front end's cost. We measured this: compiling LSim's \textasciitilde350-line source (17 loops, as audited) with all five off takes $2.03\pm0.08$\,ms of \texttt{compile-form} time; with all five on, $5.06\pm0.10$\,ms (mean $\pm$ sample stddev, 8 fresh-process trials, same machine as RQ2). This $2.49\times$ ratio is front-end-only---it excludes SBCL's downstream native-code compilation, dominating build time identically---and the overhead (about 3\,ms for 350 lines) a developer wouldn't notice in an edit-compile-test loop.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
We evaluate our work by answering eight research questions. The RQ2 microbenchmarks were measured on an AMD Ryzen 5 7430U (single-threaded) with SBCL 2.6.0 (1\,GB dynamic space). To reduce between-invocation drift (a laptop-class part suffers thermal/scheduler variation trial-to-trial stddev doesn't capture), the harness runs a three-second preflight warm-up once, three warm-up calls per workload before timing, and a two-second idle cooldown after every workload, preventing thermal bias. Each workload is five timed calls with a forced collection before each, reported as mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation, with per-call allocation read from the runtime's bytes-consed counter. The ``Off'' column disables \emph{every} optimization flag (transient conversion, scalar replacement, numeric specialization, dynamic-extent closures, optimized constructors); ``On'' enables all five, i.e.\ the pipeline a deployed build runs, not transient conversion alone. RQ7 confirms disabled emission is byte-stable vs.\ the pre-work compiler (\texttt{v0.1.1}). We verified by inspecting emitted code which of the other four flags fire, since crediting the wrong mechanism would misattribute our contribution: scalar replacement and optimized constructors never fire (no record classes); numeric specialization fires on every workload but word-count, unboxing each loop counter---never the accumulator, whose type is unprovable; dynamic-extent closures fire on the two \texttt{reduce}-based workloads, stack-allocating the literal fold function. The reported numbers thus reflect transient conversion's effect plus these verified contributions, not conversion alone or five optimizations compounding uniformly. The RQ5 ablation, RQ6's LSim figures, and RQ7's LSim world-guard comparison retain the original development-machine measurements (Ryzen 9 5900X, SBCL 2.6.0, best-of-3 or mean-of-5); RQ7's \texttt{v0.1.1} dict-loop comparison was re-measured on the RQ2 machine. Deterministic allocation ratios agree across old and new measurements where both exist.

\textbf{RQ3: Tier-2 inference on recursive functions.} The fixed-point inference of §\ref{sec:tier2} is validated at the \emph{analysis} level: the inference suite verifies summaries converge for a self-recursive accumulator pattern and for mutual recursion (\texttt{is-even?}/\texttt{is-odd?}). The bounded-iteration fallback is implemented (§\ref{sec:tier2}) but not exercised by a non-convergent test case; tested patterns converge within the iteration bound. We report no end-to-end recursion speedups, because inferred summaries do not yet feed the \emph{conversion} client: by the alignment discipline, an update chain must consist of operations the rewriter can replace with transient counterparts, and a user-defined helper has none---closing this gap is discussed as future work in §\ref{sec:discussion}.

Conversion removes 8.7--11$\times$ of allocation and closes 75--79\% of the wall-time gap to handwritten mutable Common Lisp: the dict loop goes from 35.6$\times$ slower than a hash-table loop to 9.6$\times$; the residual is generic dispatch and boxing, which transients do not address. First, the transient rewrite's output is exactly what a Clojure programmer would write by hand (§\ref{sec:background}), with a free entry guard (RQ7); the numeric-specialization win (every row but word-count) is a separate pipeline benefit a manual port wouldn't get without hand-added types. The ``On'' column is thus a fair end-to-end automation measure, not just zero-cost transients. We checked this against real Clojure (200k dict assocs, 1M vector conjs, persistent vs.\ transient, OpenJDK/GraalVM 25, same machine as RQ2, 5 fresh-process trials): hand-written transients get 4.4--5.2$\times$ (dict, mean 4.9$\times$) and 3.3--4.9$\times$ (vector, mean 4.1$\times$)---the same range as FOL's automatic 3.7--3.8$\times$, not higher, since the manual port skips the entry guard and dispatch overhead our fast path carries---corroborating that automatic conversion lands near manual, not merely resembling it syntactically. Second, small-N loops with 3 operations per boundary are modest wins (1.34--1.37$\times$), where wrapper transients' O(n) boundaries would have lost (RQ5).

\textbf{RQ4: Profitability heuristic.} Conversion cost has two axes, which should not be conflated. The first---\emph{operations per transient/persistent boundary}---is addressed by edit-tagged transients: as Table~\ref{tab:microbenchmarks} shows, even three-update boundaries are modest wins (1.34--1.37$\times$). The second is the \emph{initial size} of an accumulator starting from an already-populated collection: entering/leaving the transient protocol has constant cost that amortizes once enough structure is touched. Benchmarking a fixed number of updates against varying initial sizes locates the crossover at size 16 for dictionaries and 12 for vectors. For loops whose accumulator begins from a \emph{non-empty} collection, the compiler ANDs the world guard with a runtime size check, \texttt{(< threshold (count acc))}, checked once at loop entry. Accumulators grown from an empty literal---the common, most profitable case---are always converted: since their initial count never exceeds a threshold, guarding on initial size would make the fast path unreachable where it pays most. The measured worst cases for unguarded empty-init loops are break-even to modest wins (Table~\ref{tab:microbenchmarks}), so no iteration-count guard is emitted.

\textbf{RQ5: Ablation: wrapper vs edit-tagged transients} (original development machine). To validate our cost model, we compared our edit-tagged implementation to a wrapper-based one (simulated by disabling reads). Edit-tagged transients improve speedups from 2.30× to 4.14× for dictionaries and 5.60× to 7.52× for vectors on large loops; for small-N loops, they turn a \textasciitilde1.0× result into a 1.16× win and enable conversion of read-heavy loops previously impossible (unconvertible → 1.38×). This confirms lowering the boundary cost from O(n) to O(1)/O(32) is critical.

\textbf{RQ6: Whole-program benchmarks.} Microbenchmarks isolate the accumulation loop; a whole program dilutes the win with surrounding work it does not touch. We measured three programs spanning accumulation-dominated to accumulation-incidental (Table~\ref{tab:programs}, same machine and protocol as RQ2; LSim as noted).

\emph{Word-count} (tally 200k tokens over 997 keys into a dict, via a \texttt{reduce} that reads and updates the accumulator each step) is accumulation-dominated: conversion yields \textbf{3.5$\times$} wall-time and \textbf{17$\times$} allocation, and the converted code runs within \textbf{4.2$\times$} of a hand-written mutable hash-table loop---the read-heavy fold edit-tagged transients enable. Unlike other workloads, word-count has no loop counter, so numeric specialization doesn't apply; its fold function qualifies for dynamic-extent allocation, though that's not the source of the dict's savings. This is the clearest evidence that a whole program's runtime, not just an isolated loop, improves.

\emph{Quicksort} (partition by building two fresh child vectors in one two-accumulator loop, then recurse) is the algorithmic tier: \textbf{1.7$\times$} wall-time and \textbf{3.3$\times$} allocation. The dilution is honest---each level does unconverted reads, a \texttt{concat}, and recursion---and conversion fires at every recursion level; the partition loop's index counter is numeric-specialized too, as in the microbenchmarks. A destructive array sort (\texttt{cl:sort}, a different algorithm over unboxed storage) is another 16.6$\times$ faster, locating persistent quicksort's residual cost in representation, not just allocation.

The \emph{same} quicksort in the in-place-swap style---threading the input vector through the loop and updating it with \texttt{assoc}---is \emph{refused}: the threaded accumulator fails freshness (the caller's argument, not a fresh allocation), and \texttt{assoc} is not in the vector op-gate (only \texttt{conj!}). This is the alignment discipline drawing its line inside one algorithm: the profitable style converts, the inconvertible style does not.

On LSim, enabling world guards had no measurable impact on performance (1.02× with vs. without, within noise).

\textbf{RQ8: Live invalidation.} We tested the invalidation mechanism by running a converted loop, redefining \texttt{assoc} with side effects, and re-running it. The same function object fell back to the slow path on its next entry---per the entry-level visibility contract (§\ref{sec:soundness})---producing identical results to the original, with world-stats counters confirming only \texttt{assoc}-dependent regions were invalidated.

\textbf{Applicability beyond FOL.} To estimate how often the target pattern occurs in real code, we ran a reader-level corpus analyzer (released with our artifact) over 29 open-source Clojure projects (2{,}905 files, sharing FOL's persistent collections and \texttt{loop}/\texttt{recur}/\texttt{reduce} idioms). Of 1{,}442 loop/reduce accumulator sites, 12.7\% rebuild a \emph{map} and 21.9\% grow a \emph{vector/set/list} each iteration---about a third (34.6\%) matching the targeted shape. This is a \emph{necessary}-condition proxy only: the analyzer checks syntactic rebuild shape, not the classifier's actual gates (§\ref{sec:analysis}, §\ref{sec:opgates})---freshness, no-aliasing, and op-gate clearance. A single disqualifying use fails a loop, so the qualifying fraction is smaller, by an unmeasured amount (porting the classifier to Clojure's AST is future work). Clojure is a proxy, not a measurement of FOL, and reader-level detection both undercounts (helper-built initializers) and overcounts (relative to true qualification); we read 34.6\% as an order-of-magnitude upper bound, not an incidence estimate.

\textbf{Threats to Validity.} \emph{Construct:} the microbenchmarks are synthetic and isolate the accumulation pattern; the small-N rows bound the worst case, not realistic mixes. \emph{Internal:} timings are wall-clock on a desktop OS; we mitigate with five runs, forced collections, and mean $\pm$ standard deviation, and the deterministic allocation ratios corroborate the timing ratios. Within-run standard deviation does not capture between-invocation drift: an earlier harness version, lacking the warm-ups and cooldown now in §\ref{sec:eval}, showed workload means shift by up to $\sim$5\% across sessions; the reported figures use the mitigated harness, but residual session-to-session variation is expected, so single-decimal time ratios should be read with that in mind. Allocation ratios reproduce exactly regardless of session, hence more load-bearing. The profitability thresholds (16/12), measured on one machine, are constants, not universal. \emph{External:} one language and one end-to-end application (LSim); the Clojure corpus is a proxy and upper bound, not a precise estimate (§\ref{sec:eval}); the ablation (RQ5) and LSim figures (RQ6--RQ7) used the original development machine rather than RQ2's, except RQ7's \texttt{v0.1.1} comparison; and all numbers come from two x86-64/SBCL machines, so we claim nothing about other architectures or Lisp implementations.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{DVI does not convert---and why.} The DVI benchmark accumulates through a user-defined helper, \texttt{(add-item cart price)}. Tier-2 inference (§\ref{sec:tier2}) determines \texttt{add-item} threads its accumulator linearly, but conversion cannot act on that: by alignment, the rewriter rewrites operations it can replace with transient counterparts, never reaching \texttt{add-item}'s interior \texttt{assoc}. DVI remains unconverted, keeping the pathological GC behavior motivating this work. Closing this gap requires either inlining helpers at the update position (concurrent, anonymized work takes this approach for records) or specializing transient helper variants; both preserve alignment, the rewritten body visible to op gates.

\textbf{Novelty, and why an open world.} We claim no new escape analysis; proving uniqueness for in-place update is classic \cite{hudak1985aggregate}. What is new is the \emph{assembly} and its target---automatic transient conversion in a transpiled, latently-typed language with no optimizing IR, JIT, or type system. The open world is not incidental; it creates the problem the world guard solves. A JVM language like Clojure has the same allocation pattern but doesn't redefine classes mid-run, and its JIT performs escape analysis, deoptimizing when assumptions break; our world guard wouldn't earn its keep there. A static language like Koka attacks the same cost oppositely, with reference counting and borrowing \cite{reinking2021perceus,lorenzen2023functional} presupposing type information FOL lacks. The gap we address---transient conversion in an \emph{open-world, untyped, JIT-less} setting---is what those systems leave open; the mechanisms (name-resolution-faithful summaries, region invalidation, safe-by-abort emit-time rewriting), not FOL, are what transfer to similarly-positioned transpiled dynamic languages.

\textbf{Profitability.} Our small-N wins are modest, bounded by the transient machinery's constant-factor costs. The profitability heuristic (RQ4) confines conversion of populated-collection accumulators to sizes above the measured crossover (16 for dictionaries, 12 for vectors); accumulators grown from an empty literal---the most profitable shape---are always converted. The residual exposure is an empty-init loop performing few updates; measured cases are break-even to modest wins, so we accept it rather than an iteration-count guard.

\textbf{Completeness.} Our transient sets are wrapper-style and don't support reads; transient vectors only support \texttt{conj!}. Op gates make these completeness limitations, not correctness issues: the system refuses to convert loops using unsupported operations.

This work represents the first three "invalidation ladder" rungs, including inferred summaries. Future work could add monotonic redefinitions (e.g., adding a generic-function method that doesn't affect existing dispatch) and tripwire-based recompilation.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Perceus/FBIP.} Reinking et al. \cite{reinking2021perceus} and Lorenzen \& Leijen \cite{lorenzen2023functional} introduced compile-time lifetime analysis and ownership types enabling FBIP. Our work achieves this in a dynamically typed language without type-system annotations, handling a live, redefinable image's complexities. At the mechanism level, they converge more than the type-system framing suggests: Perceus's reuse analysis inserts a reuse-token check so a freed cell's storage can be claimed by an immediately-following allocation; our edit-tagged transients use an analogous token (the edit tag, §\ref{sec:edittagged}) to decide in-place-mutate-vs-copy at each write. The difference: where the check lives. Perceus pairs a static \emph{may-reuse} analysis with a per-cell runtime check, while we pair a static \emph{must-be-unique} proof with a token minted per transient region and never rechecked per write---trading Perceus's per-cell runtime cost for a per-region compile-time one.

\textbf{Clojure.} Our transient protocol builds on the edit-token design pioneered by Hickey in Clojure \cite{hickey2009clojure}. The key difference: Clojure's transients are manual, opt-in, and potentially unsafe, whereas our system is automatic and sound. Our work draws on the persistent-data-structure literature of Bagwell \cite{bagwell2001ideal} and Steindorfer \& Vinju \cite{steindorfer2015optimizing}.

\textbf{World Age.} Julia's world age \cite{belyakova2020jit} invalidates code from changes to method tables. As noted in §\ref{sec:soundness}, our work differs in what it protects (uniqueness facts vs. dispatch), granularity (region vs. method), and invalidation (dependency tracking vs. a monotonic counter), avoiding runtime recompilation.

\textbf{Escape Analysis.} Our work descends from escape analysis \cite{blanchet2003escape, choi1999escape, stadler2014partial}, but our client differs: unlike stack allocation, we target uniqueness-at-death to enable persistent-data-structure mutation. Unlike interprocedural analyses, our boundary policy relies on trusted, name-resolution-faithful summaries---more practical in a dynamic language where whole-program analysis is infeasible.

\textbf{Linear and Uniqueness Types.} Unique, unaliased values date back to linear and uniqueness types \cite{wadler1990linear}. Systems like Clean and Rust use type systems to enforce these properties; our work provides uniqueness's benefits for a common pattern without imposing type-system obligations.

\textbf{Runtime uniqueness and the aggregate update problem.} Establishing that an aggregate is unaliased at update time predates persistent tries: Hudak and Bloss's \emph{aggregate update problem} \cite{hudak1985aggregate} sought uniqueness-at-death for functional arrays, combining abstract interpretation with run-time reference counts. Two designs make the uniqueness check dynamic: Swift's copy-on-write collections consult the reference count per mutation via \texttt{isKnownUniquelyReferenced} \cite{swift2023cow}; Lean's runtime reuses reference-count-one cells via compiler-inserted reset/reuse pairs \cite{ullrich2019beans}. Our classifier is the static counterpart: it proves uniqueness at compile time, eliminating the per-operation check---at the price of conservatism and the world guards a dynamic check doesn't need.

\textbf{Deforestation.} The "reduce init-threading" concept (§\ref{sec:chains}) is a form of deforestation \cite{wadler1990deforestation}, where an intermediate structure (the inner reduce's result) is eliminated by threading the accumulator through it.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We present a system for automatically and safely converting accumulation loops into in-place updates in a dynamic, persistent-first language. Combining a uniqueness-at-death analysis, a construction-aligned rewriter, and a lightweight guard for soundness under live redefinition, we achieve performance gains on targeted workloads. Our honest cost model and negative results delineate the technique's effectiveness boundaries: edit-tagged transients are crucial for profitability. The two-tier summary design reaches interprocedurally into user code, including helpers; future work extends inference to multi-clause, higher-order functions, plus monotonic-redefinition and tripwire-recompilation rungs.

\section{Proof Sketch for the Soundness of Conversion Theorem}

The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} proceeds by bisimulation. We define an operational semantics for the original (Source) and transformed (Target) programs and show their observational equivalence. Throughout we assume A1--A3 (§\ref{sec:formal}): A2 justifies each per-operation step below, and Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier} supplies the uniqueness invariant that the relation maintains.

\subsection{Semantics}

We model program execution with a state $\langle e, \rho, \sigma \rangle$, where $e$ is the expression, $\rho$ is an environment from names to locations, and $\sigma$ maps locations to values.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Values:} $v ::= l | n | \dots$ where $l$ is a location in the store.
    \item \textbf{Store:} $\sigma: l \mapsto v$. Collections are heap-allocated objects. A persistent map is $\text{Map}(\text{root})$. A transient map is $\text{TMap}(\text{root}, \text{tok})$, where $\text{tok}$ is a unique ownership token.
    \item \textbf{Source Semantics (Persistent):} An update \texttt{(assoc m k v)} evaluates to a \emph{new} location $l'$ for a new map. The store $\sigma$ is extended to $\sigma' = \sigma \cup \{l' \mapsto \text{Map}(\text{new\_root})\}$. The original map at $\rho(m)$ is unchanged.
    \item \textbf{Target Semantics (Transient):} An update \texttt{(assoc! m k v)} \emph{mutates} the map at $\rho(m)$. If the node to change has the correct token, it's modified in-place; otherwise, it's copied, tagged, and mutated. The store $\sigma$ becomes $\sigma'$, with the object at $\rho(m)$ modified. \texttt{(persistent! m)} "freezes" the transient, creating a new persistent map and clearing the token.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Bisimulation Relation}

We define a bisimulation relation $\approx$ between a Source state $S = \langle e_S, \rho_S, \sigma_S \rangle$ and a Target state $T = \langle e_T, \rho_T, \sigma_T \rangle$. We write $S \approx T$ if:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item $e_S$ is syntactically identical to $e_T$ outside the converted loop. Inside the loop, $e_S$ and $e_T$ correspond via transformation rules (e.g., \texttt{assoc} vs. \texttt{assoc!}).
    \item The environments $\rho_S$ and $\rho_T$ map the same variables to corresponding locations, except for the accumulator $acc$.
    \item The stores $\sigma_S$ and $\sigma_T$ are equivalent for locations reachable from $\rho_S$ and $\rho_T$, according to a value relation $\approx_v$.
\end{enumerate}

The value relation $v_S \approx_v v_T$ holds if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $v_S$ and $v_T$ are identical scalars.
    \item $v_S$ is a persistent collection $\text{Map}(r_S)$ and $v_T$ is a persistent collection $\text{Map}(r_T)$, structurally identical.
    \item $v_S$ is the persistent accumulator $\text{Map}(r_S)$ at location $l_{acc\_S}$, and $v_T$ is the transient accumulator $\text{TMap}(r_T, \text{tok})$ at $l_{acc\_T}$, structurally identical (ignoring the token).
\end{itemize}

Crucially, for the accumulator $acc$, $\rho_S(acc)$ must be the \emph{only} reference to the map there---guaranteed by the uniqueness-at-death analysis.

\subsection{Proof Sketch by Induction on Evaluation Steps}

If $S \approx T$ and the Source program takes a step $S \rightarrow S'$, then the Target program can take a step $T \rightarrow T'$ such that $S' \approx T'$.

\textbf{Base Case: Loop Initialization.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Source: \texttt{(loop [acc (hash-map)] ...)} evaluates to a state where $\rho_S(acc)$ points to a fresh, unaliased map $m_S$.
    \item Target: \texttt{(loop [acc (transient (hash-map))] ...)} evaluates to a state where $\rho_T(acc)$ points to a fresh transient map $m_T$ with a new token.
    \item The states are related: $m_S \approx_v m_T$, and the uniqueness property holds in the Source.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Inductive Step: Inside the Loop.} We consider the evaluation of the loop body.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Case 1: Read operation \texttt{(get acc k)}.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Source: Reads from the persistent map at $\rho_S(acc)$.
        \item Target: Reads from the transient map at $\rho_T(acc)$. Our transient-aware read operations correctly interpret the mix of original and copied-on-write nodes.
        \item Equivalent structures return equivalent values. The resulting states $S'$ and $T'$ are in relation.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Case 2: Update operation \texttt{(recur (assoc acc k v))}.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Source: \texttt{(assoc acc k v)} evaluates to a \emph{new} location $l_S'$ with a new map $m_S'$. The old map at $\rho_S(acc)$ is now garbage (by the last-use property). The \texttt{recur} rebinds $acc$ to $l_S'$. The uniqueness property is maintained for the new value.
        \item Target: \texttt{(assoc! acc k v)} \emph{mutates} the transient map at $\rho_T(acc)$ in-place. The \texttt{recur} rebinds $acc$ to the same location $\rho_T(acc)$.
        \item The new map $m_S'$ and the mutated map at $\rho_T(acc)$ are structurally equivalent, so $S'$ and $T'$ remain in relation, invariant maintained.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Case 3: Other uses of \texttt{acc}.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item No other use forms occur. The bisimulation is defined only for programs satisfying \emph{Qualifies}, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier} every occurrence of $acc$ is one of the sanctioned forms covered by Cases 1--2 or a loop exit. The burden of excluding alias-creating uses is discharged by Lemma~\ref{lem:classifier}, not assumed here: were an unsanctioned use such as \texttt{(some-func acc)} with an unknown \texttt{some-func} present, the loop wouldn't qualify, so no transformation---and no proof obligation---occurs.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Inductive Step: Loop Exit.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Source: The loop exits, returning the final persistent accumulator $m_S$.
    \item Target: The expression is wrapped with \texttt{(persistent! acc)}, which evaluates the transient $m_T$ to a new persistent map $m_T'$.
    \item Since $m_S \approx_v m_T$ before the exit, the final results $m_S$ and $m_T'$ are structurally identical and observationally equivalent to the rest of the program.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Conclusion.} By induction, for any number of evaluation steps, the Source and Target programs remain in relation. Since the initial states are related and the relation preserves termination and values, the transformed program is observationally equivalent to the original. The soundness rests on the guarantees from the uniqueness-at-death classifier.
